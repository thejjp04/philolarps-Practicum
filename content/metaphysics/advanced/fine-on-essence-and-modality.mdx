The last article closed on a question it could not answer. If full grounds necessitate what they ground, where does the necessity come from? The suggestion was that it comes from essence, from what things are, but only on a conception of essence that does not itself reduce to necessity. This article supplies that conception. It follows Kit Fine's argument in "Essence and Modality" (1994) that essence cannot be defined in modal terms, using the pair the last article used against modal analyses of ground: Socrates and his singleton set.

The beginner article on essence and accident gave Fine's case a paragraph and promised the rest for later. For most of the twentieth century, analytic philosophers took it that to call a property essential to a thing is to say that the thing could not exist without it. Fine argued that this gets the order of explanation backwards. Essence, understood as what a thing is, explains necessity, and if he is right then the possible worlds apparatus of the intermediate tier records the consequences of essence rather than giving an account of it.

## The modal account

The beginner tier defined an essential property as one a thing could not have lacked while existing. The intermediate article on possible worlds gave that definition its standard precise form.

<Definition term="Modal account of essence">
The view that for a property to be essential to an object is for the object to have it necessarily: to have it at every possible world at which the object exists. An object's essence, on this view, is the collection of properties it has in every world in which it exists, and there is nothing more to being essential than this modal fact.
</Definition>

The account is a piece of de re modality. It takes an object to the worlds, asks what is true of it at each, and inherits both the clarity and the commitments of that procedure. It is also what the arguments of the last half century about essentialism mostly assumed. When Quine objected that necessity attaches to things only under descriptions, and when Kripke replied that a person could not have had different parents, both were arguing about which de re necessities hold. Neither questioned that settling the necessities would settle the essences.

The existence clause matters. Without it, the account would make Socrates essentially human only if he is human at every world, including the worlds where he does not exist and so is not anything. The account is therefore usually stated conditionally: a property is essential to an object when, necessarily, if the object exists then it has the property. Fine's examples are aimed at this conditional form, and the condition does nothing to block them.

Two directions should be kept apart. The account claims, first, that every essential property is had necessarily, and second, that every property had necessarily is essential. The first direction is accepted by nearly everyone, Fine included: whatever belongs to what a thing is, the thing could not have been without. The case against the modal account is a case against the second direction, the claim that necessity is enough.

## Socrates and his singleton

Fine's central example uses elementary set theory. For any object there is a set whose only member is that object, its singleton. Sets are the paradigm abstract objects, and the beginner article on the abstract and the concrete used impure sets, those with concrete members, as a test of where the line between the two categories falls. Singleton Socrates is as impure as a set can be, since everything about which set it is comes from Socrates.

On the standard picture of sets, the singleton exists at exactly the worlds where Socrates exists, and at each of them Socrates belongs to it. So it is necessary that if Socrates exists, he is a member of singleton Socrates. The modal account then delivers a verdict its defenders did not intend.

<Argument name="The singleton argument">
  <Premise>If the modal account is correct, any property an object has at every world at which it exists is essential to it.</Premise>
  <Premise>At every world at which Socrates exists, he is a member of singleton Socrates.</Premise>
  <Premise>Being a member of singleton Socrates is not essential to Socrates: nothing in what Socrates is, a particular human being, involves sets at all.</Premise>
  <Conclusion>The modal account is incorrect, because necessity is not sufficient for essence.</Conclusion>
</Argument>

The argument is valid, and the second premise is a piece of set theory that the modal theorist has no independent reason to deny. Everything rests on the third, and Fine's support for it comes from comparing the case with its converse. It is essential to the singleton that Socrates is its only member, since that is what makes it the set it is: a set has no more to its identity than which members it has. Socrates belonging to the set and the set containing Socrates are, modally, one connection seen from two ends. At every world where either exists, both exist and the membership holds. If the modal account were correct, the two essentialist claims would stand or fall together. They do not. One states what the set is, and the other states a fact about the man that holds because of what the set is.

The case has relatives, and they show that the problem is structural rather than a quirk of set theory.

Take distinctness. Socrates is distinct from the Eiffel Tower, and necessarily so, since what is distinct at one world is not identical at another. So at every world where Socrates exists he is distinct from the Tower, and the modal account makes being distinct from the Eiffel Tower part of his essence. But nothing about being a human being, or about being this human being, concerns a structure that would not be built for more than two thousand years after his death.

Take any necessary truth. Being such that two and two make four is a property Socrates has at every world where he exists, so the modal account counts it essential to him, and equally to the Eiffel Tower and to the number seven. A defender might shrug properties like this off as trivial. The singleton case cannot be shrugged off that way. Being a member of singleton Socrates is specific to Socrates, it is necessary, and it is still no part of what he is.

The general lesson is that necessity is indifferent to the source of a connection. When two things are necessarily connected, the modal profile records that the connection holds wherever they exist. It does not record which of the two the connection flows from, and a claim about essence is a claim about exactly that.

## Real definition

If essence is not the collection of necessary properties, something has to be said about what it is. Fine's answer was to return to the older conception that the modal account had displaced, on which the essence of a thing is what its definition gives.

The word "definition" needs care. In its everyday sense it is linguistic: a dictionary defines a word by saying what it means. The older sense is not about words.

<Definition term="Real definition">
A definition of a thing rather than of a word: an account of what the thing is, as opposed to a nominal definition, which says what a term means. A real definition of singleton Socrates says that it is the set whose sole member is Socrates; a nominal definition of "vixen" says that the word applies to female foxes.
</Definition>

The two come apart in both directions. A word can have a nominal definition while there is nothing for it to define, as with "unicorn". A thing can have a real definition that nobody has written down, or that has to be found out rather than stipulated. Nor need every object have a definition that anyone could write out in full. The view requires only that there be a fact about what the object is, and that some truths hold because of it.

Aristotle drew the distinction. In the second book of the *Posterior Analytics* he separates an account of what a name signifies from an account of what a thing is, and in the *Topics* he characterises a definition as a phrase signifying the essence. His phrase for essence, *to ti ên einai*, translates literally as "the what it was to be".

<Definition term="Aristotelian essence">
Essence understood as what a thing is, the content of its real definition, rather than as the collection of properties the thing has necessarily. On this conception the essential properties are those that belong to the thing's nature, and the thing's necessary properties are a wider class that includes whatever its nature, together with the natures of other things, makes unavoidable.
</Definition>

The beginner definition of essence had two halves: the collection of essential properties, taken as an answer to the question of what the thing is. The modal account keeps the first half and reads it modally. The Aristotelian conception keeps the second half and lets it decide the first.

Aristotle also had a category for properties that are not essential and yet go with a kind without exception. In the *Topics* he calls something a property (*idion*, traditionally "proprium") of a kind when it belongs to all and only its members without stating what they are, and his example is being capable of learning grammar: every human being has the capacity and nothing else does, but it is not what it is to be human. The later Aristotelian tradition held that propria flow from the essence, so that a thing has them necessarily without their being part of what it is. That is already the gap Fine identified.

Fine's paper "Senses of Essence" (1995) draws the gap more finely. The constitutive essence of a thing is what its real definition states. Its consequential essence adds whatever follows from that by logic alone. Both are narrower than the modal profile. Being a member of singleton Socrates is in neither of Socrates' essences, because it does not follow by logic from what Socrates is; it follows only from what Socrates is together with what the set is.

The asymmetry of the singleton case now has a home. The definition of the singleton mentions Socrates. Whatever the definition of Socrates contains, it does not mention the set. Definitions have a direction where the necessities between their objects do not, and the direction is the one that common sense and set theory agree on. Fine went on to analyse ontological dependence in roughly these terms, as a matter of one thing figuring in what another is ("Ontological Dependence", 1995), and a later article in this tier takes that analysis up.

<Aside title="Not Locke's real essence">
Locke also distinguished real from nominal essence, and the vocabulary invites confusion. His nominal essence is the abstract idea a general word stands for; his real essence is the hidden internal constitution of a thing from which its observable qualities flow, as with gold. Lockean real essence is closer to a scientific hypothesis about what causes what than to an Aristotelian definition, and Locke doubted that we could know it. Fine's real definitions are not causal and need not be hidden. The definition of a singleton is in full view.
</Aside>

## Reversing the order of explanation

The modal account explained essence by necessity. Fine proposed running the explanation the other way.

<Definition term="Essentialist source of necessity">
The view that metaphysical necessity has its source in essence: a proposition is metaphysically necessary because it is true in virtue of the natures of things. On Fine's formulation, the metaphysically necessary truths are those true in virtue of the natures of all objects whatever, and the truths that hold in virtue of the nature of some particular object form a part of them.
</Definition>

The basic locution is "it is true in virtue of the nature of $x$ that $p$", often written as a box with the object as subscript, $\Box_x p$. It is not defined in modal terms. It relates an object to a proposition, the proposition that holds because of what that object is, and unlike plain necessity it is sensitive to which object goes in the subscript. It is true in virtue of the nature of the singleton that Socrates belongs to it. It is not true in virtue of the nature of Socrates. An unrelativised box cannot draw that distinction, which is the singleton argument restated in notation.

The proposal has a model in an older account of analytic truth, on which a conceptual truth holds in virtue of what certain concepts are. Fine's suggestion is that metaphysical necessity stands to objects as conceptual necessity stands to concepts: a claim is metaphysically necessary when it holds because of the nature of the things involved. Different families of necessity then answer to different families of natures, much as the intermediate tier matched different grades of possibility to different domains of worlds.

The reversal buys three things.

The first is an explanation where the modal account offered a description. On the worlds analysis, that Socrates is human at every world where he exists is where the story ends: there is no world where he is a frog, and nothing more to say. The essentialist can say why. Being human belongs to what Socrates is, so any frog standing in his place at a world is not Socrates. Necessity becomes something that holds because of something else.

The second is that the necessities the modal account misclassified are explained without being misclassified. That Socrates belongs to his singleton is necessary because it is true in virtue of the nature of the set. That he is distinct from the Eiffel Tower can be put down to the natures of the two taken together, or to the nature of identity, without being located in Socrates' nature alone. That two and two make four holds in virtue of the nature of the numbers. On this view every necessity has a source, and the source need not be the object the necessity is about.

The third is a connection with the last article. Grounding necessitarianism held that full grounds necessitate what they ground, and left open why they should. If the existence of Socrates grounds the existence of his singleton, the essentialist can say why the first necessitates the second: it lies in the nature of the singleton to exist whenever its member does. On this picture, the natures of things underwrite the necessary connections that grounding relies on. The two notions should not be merged, since grounding relates facts to facts while essence concerns what objects are, and how exactly they connect is still disputed. What they share is the feature that matters here, a direction that bare necessity lacks.

Bob Hale's *Necessary Beings* (2013) develops a theory of this kind at book length, arguing that metaphysical necessities derive from the essences of things and putting the view to work on questions about which things exist necessarily.

## Saving the modal account

The modal theorist has two lines of reply: repair the account so that the counterexamples drop out, or question whether the alternative is intelligible. The repair comes first.

<Objection>
Every property in Fine's cases is gerrymandered. Being a member of a singleton, being distinct from a tower and being such that two and two make four are not properties that carve a thing at its joints, and the modal account need not range over them. Restrict the account to the right properties and the counterexamples vanish. One version restricts it to intrinsic properties, since membership and distinctness are relational. A more developed version is Nathan Wildman's sparse modalism (2013), which restricts it to sparse properties, the comparatively few that make for genuine similarity between things, as against the abundant properties that any predicate, however artificial, can supply. An essential property is then a sparse property that a thing has at every world at which it exists. Being a member of singleton Socrates is not sparse, so it is not essential, and the account keeps its reductive ambition.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The repair works on Socrates' side of the case and fails on the set's side. Restricting to intrinsic or sparse properties removes being a member of the singleton from Socrates' essence, but it equally removes having Socrates as sole member from the singleton's essence, since that property is no more intrinsic and no more sparse than its converse. Yet it is the clearest example of an essential property in the whole discussion. If anything belongs to what a set is, which members it has does. The restricted account therefore does not recover the asymmetry; it removes both halves of it.

The intrinsic version has a further cost. The origin properties Kripke defended, such as coming from particular parents or being made from a particular block of wood, are relational, so restricting to intrinsic properties would remove the very essentialist claims the modal account was used to state. The sparse version can avoid this by counting some relational properties as sparse, or by treating abstract objects separately. Both moves are open. But each owes a reason why having Socrates as a member makes the cut, and the only reason in view is that it is what the set is. At that point the modal criterion is no longer doing the work. A prior grasp of essence is choosing which properties to feed it.
</Reply>

Refinements of the modal account continue to be proposed, and the dispute is not closed. The difficulty they share is the one the reply identifies: a filter strong enough to exclude Fine's cases has to know in advance which side each necessary connection flows from.

## The charge of obscurity

The second line of reply accepts the counterexamples and questions the alternative.

<Objection>
The modal account's notions are well understood. Possible worlds come with a semantics, a logic and a practice of testing claims against imagined cases. "True in virtue of the nature of" comes with none of that. To take it as primitive is to explain the clear by the obscure, and the history is not encouraging: Molière mocked the scholastic habit of explaining why opium puts people to sleep by citing its dormitive virtue. Quine already found de re necessity too obscure to accept, and a primitive notion of a thing's nature asks for more than de re necessity did.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The comparison is not between an obscure notion and a clear one. The intermediate article on possible worlds closed by noting that the worlds analysis either uses modal notions to explain modality, in which case it is a model rather than a reduction, or takes worlds to be concrete, at great ontological cost. The modal account of essence inherits whichever of those it chooses.

Nor is the essentialist notion idle. Mathematicians define sets, groups and functions, and the definitions settle what the objects are, so that "it lies in the nature of the empty set to have no members" is about as clear as a metaphysical claim gets. Knowledge of essence is often easy for the same reason: you know the essence of a set by knowing which set it is. Defenders of the view, E. J. Lowe among them, have argued that knowledge of essence comes first and knowledge of what is possible derives from it. The dormitive virtue was empty because it restated the effect it was meant to explain. A real definition of the singleton does not restate the set's modal profile; it is what explains it.
</Reply>

A harder objection grants that essence is intelligible and asks whether it can explain necessity at all. Simon Blackburn put it as a dilemma in "Morals and Modals" (1987), aimed at any attempt to say what makes necessary truths necessary.

<Argument name="Blackburn's dilemma">
  <Premise>An explanation of why a truth is necessary must cite some further fact as its source.</Premise>
  <Premise>If the cited fact is itself necessary, its necessity is left unexplained, and the explanation presupposes the kind of thing it was meant to explain.</Premise>
  <Premise>If the cited fact is contingent, then it could have failed, and had it failed the truth it explains would not have held; so that truth was not necessary after all.</Premise>
  <Conclusion>No fact can explain why a necessary truth is necessary.</Conclusion>
</Argument>

The essentialist takes the first horn, and Hale, among others, argued that it is not the trap it looks like. Essentialist truths are necessary: if it is true in virtue of the nature of the singleton that Socrates belongs to it, then necessarily it is. But every explanation stops somewhere, and the question is whether it stops in the right place. Explaining the necessity of Socrates' membership by the nature of the set moves from a necessity that is about Socrates and has no account to one that states what the set is. And there is a reason why that kind of truth could not have been otherwise that does not appeal to some further necessity: a set with a different member would be a different set, not the same set with a different nature. What it is for a thing to be the thing it is cannot vary while the thing stays the same.

Whether this ends the regress or merely announces that it ends is the real question, and it remains open. The modal account is not better placed for having no answer. On its non-reductive reading the worlds analysis does not attempt to say why some ways for things to be are not among the possible ones, and on Lewis's reductive reading the answer rests on a plurality of concrete worlds that is costly on its own account.

## Next

On Fine's view, essence is one respect in which reality has more shape than the modal facts record: what things are has a direction and a priority that necessity flattens. The next article takes up a second notion of the same sort. Some properties seem to mark genuine divisions in nature and others seem gerrymandered, and David Lewis's account of naturalness, developed by Theodore Sider into a general notion of structure, asks whether reality has a privileged description in whose terms everything else is to be explained.
