A poker goes into the fire cold and comes out hot. Nothing could be more ordinary, and stating what happened without contradicting yourself turns out to be surprisingly hard. The poker is the same poker. The poker is different from how it was. Both are obviously true, and taken at face value they say that one thing both has and lacks a property.

## Two senses of same

The ambiguity has to be cleared before anything else can be said.

<Definition term="Numerical identity">
The relation a thing bears to itself and to nothing else. Two names or descriptions pick out numerically identical things when there is one thing there, not two.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Qualitative identity">
Exact similarity. Two things are qualitatively identical when they share all their relevant properties, and there are still two of them.
</Definition>

Twin sisters can be qualitatively identical and are certainly not numerically identical. There are two of them, and one can be in Oslo while the other is in Lisbon. Conversely, the poker before and after is numerically one thing and is not qualitatively the same, because it was cold and is now hot.

Ordinary English uses "same" for both, and almost every apparent paradox about change is powered by the slide from one to the other. Once the two are separated, the description of the poker stops sounding contradictory: it is numerically one and qualitatively different, and those are compatible.

## Persistence and change

<Definition term="Persistence">
Existing at more than one time. A thing persists when it is there at an earlier time and there at a later one, and it is numerically the same thing on both occasions.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Change">
A persisting thing having a property at one time and lacking it at another. Change requires numerical identity across the two times, since otherwise there is no one thing that has altered.
</Definition>

The second definition contains the important claim. Change is not two things differing. It is one thing differing from itself, and that requires the thing to be around for both readings. Replace the poker with a different poker and nothing has changed; something has been swapped.

This is why change and identity have to be treated together. An account of change presupposes an account of what makes the later thing the same as the earlier one, and an account that is too strict makes change impossible while an account that is too loose makes it trivial.

<Aside title="The restriction to intrinsic properties">
Not every difference over time is a change in the relevant sense. When someone in Delhi grows taller than you, you cease to be taller than them, and nothing about you has altered.

Change proper is restricted to the properties a thing has in virtue of how it itself is. What is left over is sometimes called Cambridge change, and it is not a defect in the definition but a further specification of it: change is the gain or loss of an intrinsic property.
</Aside>

## The principle that generates the problem

<Definition term="Leibniz's law">
The principle that if a is identical with b, then everything true of a is true of b. Also called the indiscernibility of identicals.
</Definition>

The principle looks unassailable, and it is very close to being so. If there is one thing there, it cannot both have and lack a property, because there is nothing for the difference to be a difference between.

It is also indispensable. Every argument for distinctness in metaphysics runs through it. Show that something is true of the statue and not of the clay, and you have shown that the statue is not the clay. Take that principle away and no such argument works.

The difficulty is that change is exactly a case where something appears to be true of a thing that is not true of it.

<Argument name="The problem of change">
  <Premise>The poker at noon is numerically identical with the poker at one o'clock.</Premise>
  <Premise>The poker at noon is cold; the poker at one o'clock is not cold.</Premise>
  <Premise>If a is identical with b, then everything true of a is true of b.</Premise>
  <Conclusion>The poker at noon both is and is not cold, which is impossible.</Conclusion>
</Argument>

Something in that has to go, and each option has been defended.

## The responses

The simplest response denies that the properties are what the second premise says. Being cold, on this view, is not a property the poker has full stop; it is a property the poker has at a time. Properly stated, the poker is cold-at-noon and is also not-cold-at-one, and both of those hold of it permanently. Leibniz's law is untouched, because everything true of the thing at noon is true of the thing at one o'clock, once the temporal qualification is written into the property.

The objection to this is that it makes shape and temperature into relations to times rather than ways the poker is in itself, which seems to get the phenomenon wrong. Being bent is not a relation the poker bears to anything. David Lewis pressed this as the problem of temporary intrinsics, and it is the standard test any account has to pass.

A second response qualifies the having rather than the property. The poker has coldness at noon and hotness at one, where the at-a-time attaches to the instantiating instead of to what is instantiated. This preserves the intrinsic character of the properties, at the cost of an account of instantiation that is more complicated than it first appeared.

A third response denies the first premise, but not in the way that would make change impossible. On this view a persisting thing is extended through time as well as through space, and has different parts at different times, in the way a road has different parts in different counties. The poker at noon is one temporal part and the poker at one o'clock is another, and there is no single thing that is both cold and hot, any more than a road that is narrow in one county and wide in another is both narrow and wide.

<Objection>
The third response saves the principle by giving up the thing. If the poker at noon is a different part from the poker at one, then strictly nothing survived the fire, and what looked like one object having a history is really a series of momentary objects laid end to end. That is not an account of change; it is a denial of it.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The defender replies that the whole poker, extended through time, is a single thing, and that it is the thing which changed: it has a cold part and a hot part, and having qualitatively different parts at different times is exactly what change amounts to. The spatial analogy is meant to make this unremarkable. A poker that is thick at one end and thin at the other is not two pokers.

Whether the analogy holds is the crux, and it depends on whether time is enough like space for the comparison to be legitimate. That is why this dispute cannot be settled without a view about time, and why the intermediate tier takes the two together.
</Reply>

## What has been established

The vocabulary is now precise enough to state the puzzles rather than merely feel them. There is a difference between being one thing and being exactly alike. There is a requirement that change involve one thing across two times. There is a principle about identity that no account can simply discard.

What has not been established is which response to the problem of change is correct. Each survives, each pays a price, and the price is paid in a different currency in each case.
