"The mug is chipped." Two things are doing different jobs in that sentence. "The mug" picks out a thing. "Is chipped" says how that thing is. The division is so familiar that it takes effort to see it as a claim about the world rather than a fact about English, and the whole of this article is an attempt to say what the claim amounts to.

## Things

<Definition term="Object">
A thing that has properties without being a property of anything else: the mug, the mountain, the electron, arguably the number nine. Also called a particular, an individual, or in the older vocabulary a substance.
</Definition>

The definition is deliberately thin, and it earns its keep by what it refuses to build in. It does not say objects are made of matter, so numbers are not excluded by definition. It does not say objects are simple, so a mug with a handle qualifies. It does not say objects last, so a brief flash of light is not ruled out.

The one substantive commitment is the asymmetry. An object bears properties and is not itself borne. Whether that asymmetry is real, or whether an object is nothing more than a bundle of the properties it bears, is one of the standing disputes of the subject. The intermediate tier takes it up under the heading of substrata and bundles. For now, notice that the ordinary way of speaking assumes the asymmetry, and that assuming it is not the same as establishing it.

## Ways things are

<Definition term="Property">
A way a thing is: being chipped, having a mass of two kilograms, being taller than the fence. Properties are what predicates are true of things in virtue of, and are also called attributes, qualities, or in some contexts features.
</Definition>

Properties are individuated more finely than the things that have them. One mug has a great many properties at once, and the same property can be had by many different things. That second point is where the trouble lives.

Two mugs are both chipped. Is there one thing, chippedness, that both have? Or are there two chipped mugs and nothing else, with "chipped" being a word that applies to each of them separately? Almost the entire literature on properties descends from that question, and the ontology ladder devotes a full article to it. This article stays neutral. What is needed here is only the working distinction between a thing and how it is, which every side of that dispute accepts in some form.

<Aside title="Relations">
Being taller than the fence is not a way the mountain is all by itself. It involves two things and holds between them, and the standard name for such a property is a relation.

Some writers treat relations as a separate category from properties. Others treat properties as the special case of relations with one slot. This site takes the second line, so "property" here covers relations unless an article says otherwise. Nothing in the beginner tier turns on the choice.
</Aside>

## What connects them

Saying that the mug is chipped puts a thing and a property together. The name for that putting together is not obvious, because there are two quite different things it might mean.

<Definition term="Predication">
The linguistic operation of applying a predicate to a subject: attaching "is chipped" to "the mug" to form a sentence that can be true or false.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Instantiation">
The relation that holds in the world between a thing and a property it has, in virtue of which a predication is true. Also called exemplification, or having.
</Definition>

Keeping these apart is the single most useful discipline in this part of the subject. Predication is something speakers do. Instantiation is what makes what they do come out true. A world with no speakers still has mugs that are chipped, if there are mugs and chipping in it, so instantiation cannot be the same thing as predication.

The separation also explains why predication is a poor guide to what there is. Grammar hands out predicates freely. "Is famous", "is average", "is fictional", and "is not a mug" all attach happily to subjects. It does not follow that there is a property of being not a mug that everything except mugs instantiates. Some philosophers accept exactly that abundance and hold that every meaningful predicate corresponds to a property. Others hold that only some predicates track genuine properties, the ones that carve nature at its joints, and treat the rest as useful shorthand. The dispute has a standard label, sparse versus abundant conceptions of properties, and it recurs throughout the intermediate tier.

<Objection>
Instantiation is supposed to explain how a thing and a property are joined. But instantiation is itself a relation, so now the mug, the property, and the instantiating have to be joined. If a further relation does that, the same question arises about it, and the explanation never finishes.
</Objection>

<Reply>
This is Bradley's regress, and it is a genuine constraint rather than a knockdown. The usual response denies the first step: instantiation is not a further constituent standing alongside the mug and the property, but a primitive tie, something the mug and the property do rather than a third item needing to be attached to them. Whether that answer is principled or merely stops the regress by declaring it stopped is a fair question, and it is one reason bundle theories, which dispense with the tie by dispensing with the bearer, keep their supporters.
</Reply>

## Two kinds of property

Not all properties belong to a thing in the same way, and the distinction matters for almost everything later in the tier.

<Definition term="Intrinsic property">
A property a thing has purely in virtue of how it itself is, independently of what anything else is like. Having a mass of two kilograms, and being spherical, are the standard examples.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Extrinsic property">
A property a thing has partly in virtue of how other things are, or how it is related to them. Being the tallest mountain in Wales, being owned by Priya, and being three metres from the door are all extrinsic.
</Definition>

The test that comes most readily to mind is the lonely one. A property is intrinsic if a thing could have it while being the only thing in existence. It works well on the examples. A lone sphere is still spherical; a lone mountain is not the tallest in anything.

David Lewis offered three ways of orientating the distinction, and they are worth having in outline because they come apart. The first is about aboutness: an intrinsic property is one whose having is not about anything besides the thing itself. The second is about determination: intrinsic properties are fixed by the thing's own nature, and nothing outside it can change them without changing it. The third is about duplication: intrinsic properties are exactly those that any perfect duplicate of the thing shares.

These three sound like ways of saying one thing, and the literature does not treat them that way. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on the distinction notes that they may well pick out different notions rather than converging on a single one. That is a good early lesson: a distinction can be indispensable in practice and still resist a clean definition.

<Aside title="Why the distinction earns its place">
Change, in the next articles, is going to be defined in terms of a thing having a property at one time and lacking it at another. Without the intrinsic and extrinsic split, that definition delivers absurd results.

When a man in Delhi grows taller than you, you thereby stop being taller than him. Nothing about you has altered. If that counts as your changing, then everything changes constantly for reasons that have nothing to do with it. Restricting genuine change to intrinsic properties is how the account avoids this, and the standard name for what is left over is Cambridge change.
</Aside>

## What has been fixed

There are things, there are ways those things are, and there is whatever it is that holds between the two. This article has not said whether properties exist in addition to the things that have them, nor whether objects are anything over and above their properties. Both questions are live, and both are treated later.

What it has fixed is the vocabulary, precisely enough that those disputes can be stated. The next article uses it immediately, on the distinction between the abstract and the concrete.
