The last article asked what a cause is. This one asks what follows if everything that happens has causes of a certain thorough kind, and the thing that might not survive is us. You decide to leave the office early, and it feels as though you could have stayed. If the whole history of the universe, together with its laws, fixed that you would leave, then it is not obvious in what sense staying was ever open to you. The worry is old, but it took its sharpest modern form in the 1970s, and it is a worry about possibility: about what was really possible for you, as opposed to what is merely consistent with the description of the case.

## Determinism and its rivals

<Definition term="Determinism">
The thesis that the state of the world at any one time, together with the laws of nature, entails the state of the world at every other time. Given the past and the laws, exactly one future is possible.
</Definition>

The classic statement is Laplace's, in 1814: an intelligence that knew all the forces of nature and the position of every body at one moment could, with enough calculating power, foresee the entire future. Notice what the thesis is not. It is not the claim that everything is predictable by us, which is a claim about our limitations. It is not fatalism, the claim that things will happen whatever we do. A determinist can say that your choices are causes, that they make a difference, and that the future depends on them. Determinism says only that those choices are themselves the products of earlier states of the world and the laws.

<Definition term="Indeterminism">
The thesis that the past and the laws leave more than one future open: at least some events have no sufficient cause in the prior state of the world, and the laws give only probabilities for how things will go.
</Definition>

Whether the actual world is deterministic is an empirical matter that physics has not settled. Classical mechanics is deterministic in its usual formulation, though even there certain idealised setups produce odd results. Quantum mechanics divides by interpretation: collapse theories such as the Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber model are indeterministic, whilst Bohmian mechanics reproduces the quantum predictions with a fully deterministic dynamics. So philosophers of free will do not usually stake their position on the physics. They ask a conditional question: if determinism were true, could anyone act freely?

The question matters beyond the metaphysics because of what we ordinarily do with the answer. We blame, praise, punish and resent, and these practices seem to assume that people could have done otherwise.

<Definition term="Moral responsibility">
The status of being an appropriate target of reactive attitudes such as blame, praise, resentment and gratitude, in virtue of what one has done. To say someone is morally responsible for an act is to say that the act can be fairly held against them, or counted in their favour.
</Definition>

Free will is usually understood as whatever capacity is required for moral responsibility in this sense, though that is itself a substantive claim, as the last sections will show. Everything below turns on whether that capacity is compatible with determinism.

## Two kinds of answer

There are two questions here, and the standard map of positions comes from combining the answers.

<Definition term="Compatibilism">
The thesis that free will, and the moral responsibility that depends on it, is compatible with the truth of determinism. The compatibilist holds that an agent can act freely in a deterministic world.
</Definition>

Its opposite, incompatibilism, holds that free will requires the falsity of determinism. Incompatibilists then split. Some accept that we are free and deny determinism. Others conclude that, since either determinism or indeterminism is true and neither leaves room for the right kind of control, we are not free at all.

<Definition term="Libertarian free will">
The view that we do act freely, that free action is incompatible with determinism, and that therefore at least some human actions are not determined by the past and the laws.
</Definition>

This is the metaphysical position, not the political one, and the two should not be confused. The libertarian about free will need hold no views about taxation. What the libertarian does hold is that in a free choice the agent really could have chosen differently, with the entire past and the laws held exactly fixed.

Compatibilists have traditionally replied that this demand is confused. Hobbes and Hume thought that freedom is the absence of external obstruction: you act freely when you do what you want and nothing prevents you. A prisoner is unfree and a person strolling in a park is free, and the difference has nothing to do with whether the strolling was caused. G. E. Moore gave this a modern form in 1912 by analysing "could have done otherwise" as "would have done otherwise, if I had chosen to". On this conditional analysis the sentence is perfectly consistent with determinism, since a determined agent can truly say that had they chosen otherwise, they would have acted otherwise.

## The consequence argument

The most influential case against this comfortable picture is Peter van Inwagen's, first published in 1975 and given its full form in *An Essay on Free Will* in 1983. It is less an argument about free will than an argument about what "can" means.

<Definition term="The consequence argument">
The argument that if determinism is true, then our acts are the consequences of the remote past and the laws of nature, and since neither of those is up to us, neither are their consequences. Hence we have no choice about what we do.
</Definition>

Abbreviate "p, and nobody has, or ever had, any choice about whether p" as "Np". Let $P_0$ be a complete description of the world at some time long before anyone existed, let $L$ be the laws of nature, and let $P$ be any true proposition about what you do now.

<Argument name="The consequence argument">
  <Premise>If determinism is true, then $P_0$ and $L$ together entail $P$.</Premise>
  <Premise>It is not up to anyone what happened before they were born, so $N P_0$.</Premise>
  <Premise>It is not up to anyone what the laws of nature are, so $N L$.</Premise>
  <Premise>If $N$ holds of some propositions, it holds of whatever they entail, since nobody has a choice about the consequences of what they have no choice about.</Premise>
  <Conclusion>If determinism is true, then $N P$: nobody has any choice about what they do, so nobody acts freely.</Conclusion>
</Argument>

The fourth premise is the engine, and van Inwagen defended it using a rule: from $Np$ and $N(p \to q)$, infer $Nq$. If you cannot affect the past and cannot affect the fact that the past necessitates your act given the laws, you cannot affect your act. The thought is the same as the one that makes you doubt anyone can change the outcome of a game already played, given that the outcome follows from how it was played.

What the argument has going for it is that it does not require libertarian metaphysics to get started. It does not say that determinism feels oppressive. It says that the premises are about the same relation we use in everyday life when we ask whether something is up to us, and it applies that relation consistently.

## Compatibilist replies

There are three places to attack the argument, and each has a defender.

The first attacks the rule. Thomas McKay and David Johnson argued in 1996 that rule beta, together with its consequence that $N$ distributes over conjunction, is invalid. Suppose a fair coin is in fact not tossed, though you could have tossed it. Let $p$ be "the coin does not land heads" and $q$ be "the coin does not land tails". Nobody has any choice about $p$, since no one can make a tossed coin land heads, and the same goes for $q$. But the conjunction says the coin lands neither way, and you do have a choice about that, since you could toss it. So $Np$ and $Nq$ hold whilst $N(p \wedge q)$ fails. Van Inwagen responded by reformulating the rule, but the exchange shows the argument depends on an inference less secure than it first looks.

The second reply concerns the second and third premises. David Lewis's 1981 paper "Are We Free to Break the Laws?" distinguishes a weak thesis from a strong one. The strong thesis is that I am able to do something that would be a breaking of the laws. The weak thesis is that I am able to do something such that, if I did it, a law would have been broken, because had I done it, the past would have been slightly different, with a small miracle somewhere before my act. The compatibilist, says Lewis, need only accept the weak thesis. In a deterministic world, suppose I do not raise my hand but could have. Had I raised it, the past would have differed slightly beforehand, through a small divergence miracle relative to the actual laws. My raising my hand would not have caused that miracle, and so I would not have broken any law, but a law of the actual world would have failed in that counterfactual world. So I am able to do something such that, were I to do it, the actual laws would not have held. That is why the premise "nobody has a choice about the laws" is false on the reading the argument needs.

A reader may feel that this concedes the point whilst changing the words. It is hard to reply without begging the question, because the premise and Lewis's denial of it are both about the same notion of what is within one's power. A harder-line compatibilist, such as Helen Beebee and Alfred Mele in 2002, argues that on a Humean view of laws as summaries of the pattern of events, the laws really are partly up to us, and so the premise is simply false.

<Objection>
The conditional analysis does not capture what we mean. The prisoner who is determined to refuse the offer of escape would have escaped, had he chosen to, and so on Moore's account he could have escaped. But if his choice itself was fixed, then what he lacked was precisely the ability to choose otherwise. The analysis relocates the "could" into the "if" clause, where it was never allowed to be.
</Objection>

<Reply>
J. L. Austin's 1956 paper "Ifs and Cans" made a version of this point, and it has force against the simple analysis. Modern compatibilists have responded by dropping it. They analyse ability in terms of the agent's dispositions or reasons-responsiveness: an agent can do otherwise when, were they to have good enough reason, they would have responded to it. The ability to do otherwise is then a structural feature of the agent's mind and not a conditional that quietly assumes it. That is a more defensible theory, though whether it captures the freedom the incompatibilist wants is the very point in dispute.
</Reply>

## Alternate possibilities and responsibility

So far, the debate is about what an agent can do. A rival tradition says that this was the wrong question, because what really matters is responsibility, and responsibility does not depend on alternatives.

<Definition term="The principle of alternate possibilities">
The principle that a person is morally responsible for what they have done only if they could have done otherwise.
</Definition>

The principle is what links the consequence argument to responsibility. If responsibility requires being able to do otherwise, and determinism removes that ability, then determinism removes responsibility. Many incompatibilists simply took the principle for granted. In 1969, Harry Frankfurt published a challenge to it that has shaped the field since.

Frankfurt's example, in its usual retelling, runs as follows. Black wants Jones to shoot Smith. He has secretly installed a device in Jones's brain which, should Jones show any sign of deciding not to shoot, will force him to decide to shoot. In fact Jones, for his own reasons, decides to shoot without any interference, and the device never activates. Jones could not have done otherwise: had he wavered, Black would have intervened. Yet it seems that Jones is fully responsible for the shooting, since the device played no role in what happened.

If this is right, the principle of alternate possibilities is false, and responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise. The consequence argument might still show that determinism takes away that ability, but this would not matter, because the ability was never required. Compatibilists such as John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza have built accounts of responsibility on this foundation. What matters is that the act issues from the agent's own reasons-responsive mechanism, and not that other mechanisms were available.

<Objection>
Frankfurt cases beg the question against the libertarian. In a case where Jones's decision is the kind of thing that could be a free one, there must be some sign by which Black can tell that Jones is about to decide otherwise, and the sign comes before the decision. Jones can still do something different at that earlier point: he can fail to show the sign. This is the "flicker of freedom", a small alternative that is enough to ground responsibility.
</Objection>

<Reply>
David Widerker pressed the dilemma in 1995 and Carl Ginet and others developed it. If Jones's decision is determined by the prior sign, the case presupposes determinism and so begs the question against the incompatibilist. If it is not, then Black cannot reliably intervene in time. The Frankfurt-style defender replies with more elaborate examples in which the prior sign is not itself something the agent controls, so that no morally relevant flicker remains. Whether any such example succeeds is where the argument has stalled. It is fair to say that the cases at least show that the principle is not obviously true, and that its defenders must now argue for it.
</Reply>

There is a deeper alternative, due to P. F. Strawson's 1962 essay "Freedom and Resentment". Strawson suggested that the question "is it rational to hold people responsible if determinism is true?" is the wrong way round. Our reactive attitudes, such as resentment and gratitude, are part of what it is to be involved in relationships with other people, and a theoretical thesis could not rationally require us to give them up. Responsibility is then not something we discover by looking at metaphysics, but a practice that sets the terms in which metaphysical claims are assessed.

## The libertarian's cost

If the consequence argument stands and the compatibilist replies fail, then free will requires indeterminism. But indeterminism alone does not help. An event that merely happens by chance is not an exercise of control. This is the luck objection, and it is the main source of difficulty for libertarianism.

Imagine a decision that, given the past and the laws, has a fifty per cent chance of going one way and fifty per cent the other. Suppose it goes the first way. Nothing about the agent explains why it went that way rather than the other, since both were equally possible given everything about them. Van Inwagen's version of this uses the idea of rewinding the world to the moment before the decision. If it were replayed many times, the choice would go each way in proportion to the chances. That looks like chance, not control.

Libertarians have three main replies. Robert Kane's event-causal view holds that in genuinely difficult choices, where the agent is torn between moral and prudential reasons, there is an indeterministic effort of will, and whichever way it goes the agent has tried to bring about that outcome, so the outcome is something they were trying to do. Critics respond that trying does not remove the luck about whether the effort succeeds. A second reply, associated with Thomas Reid and in the twentieth century with Roderick Chisholm, is agent causation: the agent, as a substance, is a cause of the action without being caused to cause it by some prior event. This sidesteps the luck worry by making the agent the explanation. The price is a form of causation not reducible to events, which many find mysterious, and the question of what, if not luck, determines which way the agent causes.

The third reply is to deny the problem. Some libertarians hold that the reasons for an action can explain it without determining it, and that there is no further demand for a contrastive explanation of why one option was chosen rather than the other.

Given these costs, a fourth position gains ground. Hard incompatibilists such as Derk Pereboom accept the consequence argument, accept the luck objection, and conclude that we lack the free will required for the sort of moral responsibility that justifies blame as desert. We may keep many of our practices, but on a forward-looking basis such as deterrence and protection.

## What is at stake

Everything turns on whether the compatibilist has answered the incompatibilist, or merely changed the topic. The incompatibilist charge is that the compatibilist's freedom, however sophisticated, is not the freedom people actually care about when they ask if they are the ultimate source of their actions. The compatibilist reply is that the freedom the incompatibilist wants is either unintelligible, as the luck objection suggests, or unnecessary, as the Frankfurt cases suggest.

The question is also not only abstract. A jury, a prison system, and anyone deciding whether to forgive a friend are all implicitly committed to a view about what a person is answerable for. Which view is correct determines whether blame is something deserved or only something useful.

Whichever way these issues fall, the argument has shown how heavily the debate leans on one notion: what could have happened, given what was fixed. The dispute is about which things are held fixed and what "could" quantifies over, and that is the question of how to give a systematic account of possibility and necessity themselves, which the next article takes up through the idea of possible worlds.
