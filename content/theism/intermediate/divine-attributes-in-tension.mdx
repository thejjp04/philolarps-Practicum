The last two articles asked whether the attributes fit the world. This one asks whether they fit each other.

The question is prior to the other one and it is easy to skip. If the description is internally inconsistent, then nothing satisfies it, and no argument for or against the existence of the thing described is worth running. That is a stronger conclusion than any argument from evidence could reach, and it is available without leaving the armchair.

## Where the list comes from

<Definition term="Perfect being theology">
The method of determining God's attributes a priori, by identifying God as the greatest possible being and then working out which properties such a being would have.
</Definition>

Anselm's formula from the ontological argument article is doing double duty. There it was a premise in an argument for existence. Here it is a research programme: settle what God is like by asking what it is better to be.

Thomas V. Morris gave the influential modern statement in *Anselmian Explorations*, University of Notre Dame Press (1987). The procedure is to take a candidate property, ask whether having it is greater than lacking it, and include it if so. Knowledge is better than ignorance, so omniscience. Power is better than impotence, so omnipotence. And so on down the list.

Two things about the method matter for everything that follows.

The first is that it runs on intuitions about what is greater, and those intuitions are contested. Is it greater to be capable of suffering with those you love, or to be beyond being affected by anything? The tradition answered firmly in one direction, and a good deal of recent work answers in the other, and there is no agreed way to adjudicate. William Wainwright and Peter van Inwagen have both pressed the point that without such a way the method cannot settle disputes, which is what it was for. Wainwright has also asked whether the being it constructs is the one anybody worships.

The second is that the method assembles a list without checking that the list can be jointly satisfied. Each property is selected on its own merits. Whether the selected set is coherent is a separate question, which the method does not ask, and this article is about what happens when someone asks it.

<Definition term="Attribute incompatibility">
An argument that two or more of the properties ascribed to God cannot both be instantiated by the same being, so that nothing answers to the description and God as classically conceived does not exist.
</Definition>

The form is worth noticing because it shares the structural advantage of the problem of evil. Everything the critic needs is supplied by the theist. No claim about the world is required, so no empirical dispute can arise, and success would establish not that God happens not to exist but that nothing could satisfy the description.

Two responses are always available, and they cost different amounts. One is to show the incompatibility is only apparent, by finding a reading of the attributes on which they cohere. The other is to revise or drop one of the attributes. The second is not automatically a defeat, since the tradition's formulations were not handed down complete, but it is a concession, and how much of one depends on which property gets revised.

## Omnipotence, and what a puzzle is worth

<Definition term="Paradox of the stone">
The puzzle asking whether an omnipotent being can create a stone too heavy for it to lift, on the ground that either answer seems to identify something the being cannot do.
</Definition>

Both answers look bad. If God cannot make the stone, there is something God cannot make. If God can make it, there is something God cannot lift.

The standard reply goes back to Aquinas and is about what counts as a task. Omnipotence is power over what is possible. A description of a logically impossible state of affairs does not describe a difficult task that God fails at; it describes nothing at all, and failing to bring about nothing is not a limitation. Making a square circle is not something God cannot do, because it is not a something.

George Mavrodes applied this directly in "Some Puzzles Concerning Omnipotence", *The Philosophical Review* 72, no. 2 (1963), pp. 221-223. If the being in question is omnipotent, then the phrase "a stone too heavy for that being to lift" is self-contradictory, in the same way as "a square circle". So the request to make one is not a request to do anything. C. Wade Savage's "The Paradox of the Stone", *The Philosophical Review* 76, no. 1 (1967), pp. 74-79, is the standard fuller treatment arguing that the paradox does not establish an inconsistency.

A more careful version distinguishes a being that is omnipotent necessarily from one that happens to be omnipotent and could cease to be. For the second, both making the stone and then being unable to lift it are straightforwardly possible, at different times, and the paradox dissolves without any appeal to what counts as a task. For the first, being non-omnipotent is impossible, so the stone is impossible, and Aquinas's answer applies.

Descartes dissented, apparently holding that God's power extends over the necessary truths themselves, so that the logically impossible is not a limit either. Almost no one follows him, and the reason is that the position cannot be stated: if contradictions can be true, the claim that God can make them true does not rule anything out, and an assertion that rules nothing out says nothing.

The stone is worth having as an example of the difference between a puzzle and a problem. It is answered, the answer is not ad hoc, and it survives in popular argument long after it stopped being live. A harder case in the same area is whether God can sin. If God is essentially good then God cannot, and there is then an act available to any ordinary person that God cannot perform. The usual reply is that being able to act wrongly is not a power but a liability, and that a being incapable of it is not thereby less able. That is a reasonable answer and it is not obviously a complete one, since the ability to do otherwise is precisely what the next section but one turns out to need.

## Immutability

<Definition term="Immutability">
The doctrine that God cannot undergo change of any kind, since a perfect being could change only for the worse and a being that changes for the better was not perfect before.
</Definition>

The motivation is easy to state and hard to escape. If God is perfect, any change is a change away from perfection. The argument was regarded as decisive for most of the tradition's history.

The cost arrives quickly. A God who cannot change cannot respond, cannot come to know that something has happened, and cannot be moved by anything, which sits badly with almost every religious text the doctrine was formulated to interpret. The tradition's answer involves a further set of doctrines about God's relation to time that the advanced tier examines in detail.

The sharpest form of the tension is Norman Kretzmann's, in "Omniscience and Immutability", *The Journal of Philosophy* 63, no. 14 (1966), pp. 409-421, and it is the model of this kind of argument.

An omniscient being knows everything that is true. Some things are true at one time and false at another: it is true today that a particular event is happening, and tomorrow it will be true instead that it has happened. So an omniscient being knows one thing today and a different thing tomorrow, and a being whose knowledge differs across times has changed. Omniscience therefore entails mutability, and immutability is inconsistent with it.

Kretzmann also pressed a second version that has proved harder. Some knowledge appears to be available only from a particular position: what a person in a hospital bed knows in knowing that they are the one in pain does not seem to be available to anyone else, however complete their information. If omniscience requires knowing everything anyone knows, an omniscient being would have to be each of them.

The responses divide along the lines the previous section set out. One approach reinterprets: God knows all truths in a way that does not involve occupying any moment, so there is no succession in the knowing and no change. That preserves both attributes at the price of a substantial account of God's relation to time, and it faces the question of whether a being outside time can know what is happening now. The other approach revises: give up strict immutability, allow that God changes in relation to a changing world while remaining constant in character. That answers Kretzmann directly and gives up something the tradition treated as settled.

## Omnipresence

<Definition term="Omnipresence">
The property of being present everywhere, ascribed to God despite God's being immaterial and therefore not the kind of thing that occupies a region.
</Definition>

Augustine's formulation is that God is wholly present wherever God is present, and is not spread out by size in the way a body is, with one part here and another there. Anselm put the same point as a pair of claims that only sound contradictory: God is in no place, in the sense of being bounded by one, and is wholly in every place.

Aquinas gave the account that organised the medieval discussion, treating God as present by power, since everything is subject to God's action; by knowledge, since everything is open to God; and by essence, as the cause of each thing's existing at all. The presence is understood analogically rather than as literal location.

The interesting difficulty here is not an inconsistency but a suspicion. If omnipresence amounts to knowing what happens everywhere and being able to act everywhere, then it is not a further attribute at all. It is omniscience and omnipotence restated in spatial vocabulary, and the list is shorter than advertised. Aquinas's third channel, presence by essence, is what is supposed to prevent that collapse, and whether it succeeds depends on the account of causation behind it.

Recent work has taken the opposite route and argued that God is literally located, which raises the questions the classical view was avoiding: whether an immaterial thing can have a location, whether God and a chair can occupy the same region, and whether being everywhere requires having parts. Both directions are live, and this is one place where the standard list turns out to conceal a genuine open question rather than a settled doctrine.

## Divine freedom

<Definition term="Divine freedom">
The question whether God can act freely, given that a being who is essentially omnipotent and essentially perfectly good appears to have no alternative but to do the best available thing.
</Definition>

The tension is sharp and does not depend on anything controversial. Suppose in some situation one action is better than all the others. A being who is essentially perfectly good cannot do worse than best. So the being does that action, and could not have done otherwise, and on the most familiar account of freedom that is exactly what unfreedom is.

The problem generalises to creation. If God had reason to create, the reason applied necessarily, and a God who could not have refrained is not obviously the free creator of the tradition. If God had no reason, the choice was arbitrary, which is not much better.

Klaas Kraay's survey of the field, published in 2025, sets out four models of what divine freedom would require: being able to do otherwise, being free of external constraint, responding properly to reasons, and being the ultimate source of one's own actions. Only the first generates the problem in its acute form, and much of the response consists in arguing that the first is not what freedom requires. A being who acts from its own nature, unconstrained by anything outside itself, and perfectly responsive to reasons, has as much freedom as it makes sense to want, and the demand for available alternatives is a demand that the being sometimes act worse than it could.

Robert Adams argued in a different direction, that God may permissibly choose a good enough world rather than the best one, which restores alternatives at the cost of denying that a perfect being must maximise. The question of whether there is a best world at all, and what follows if there is not, belongs to the advanced tier, which takes it up on its own terms.

<Aside title="What all four have in common">
Every tension in this article comes from the same source, and seeing it makes the advanced material much easier.

Each attribute is an unlimited superlative, and each is also predicated of an agent: something that knows, acts, chooses, and is addressed. Superlatives taken alone are manageable. Agency taken alone is manageable. The trouble is that the concepts we have for knowing, acting, and choosing are concepts of finite beings doing those things in time, and pushing them to a limit tends to break them rather than to extend them.

That diagnosis leaves two routes, and the whole later literature is organised by the choice between them.

One is to hold the superlatives and give up the ordinary sense of the agency terms, treating everything said about God as analogical, so that God's knowing is not a magnified version of ours but something the word points at without capturing. That is the classical route, and it protects consistency by weakening what the claims assert.

The other is to hold the ordinary sense of the agency terms and trim the superlatives, so that God is genuinely responsive, genuinely in time, and unlimited in the ways a personal agent could be. That keeps the religious content vivid at the price of a God the tradition would not recognise, and it revives every argument the superlatives were introduced to answer.

Both routes have serious defenders. The advanced tier follows each of them out.
</Aside>

<Objection>
None of this is discovery. It is drafting.

Each time a problem appears, the description is amended until it stops generating one. Omnipotence turns out not to cover the impossible. Immutability turns out to permit variation in relation. Freedom turns out not to require alternatives. Presence turns out to be analogical.

A description revised whenever it fails can always be made consistent, and its consistency then tells you nothing about the world, only about the persistence of the people maintaining it. Compare a physicist who responds to every failed prediction by adjusting what the theory was supposed to have predicted.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The comparison is the right one to test, and it does not go the way the objection needs.

Adjustment under pressure is not by itself a fault, and treating it as one would condemn most successful inquiry. What distinguishes a legitimate revision from a degenerate one is whether it is independently motivated and whether it costs anything. Aquinas's restriction of omnipotence to the possible was not invented for the stone; it follows from a general account of what a power is, and it has consequences elsewhere that its holders accept. That is a principled amendment. By contrast, the appeal to reasons beyond our grasp in the previous article's skeptical theism was pressed by its critics precisely because it seemed to cost nothing and to be available whatever happened, which is the shape the objection here describes.

The revisions in this article also cost a great deal, which is the clearest evidence that they are not free. Making immutability compatible with omniscience requires either a demanding account of divine timelessness or the abandonment of a doctrine the tradition held for fifteen centuries. Making divine freedom work requires denying that freedom involves alternatives, which is a substantive and contested position in the philosophy of action with implications far outside theology. These are not costless adjustments; they are commitments that can be attacked, and they are attacked.

What should be granted is the underlying worry about method. Perfect being theology does produce its list by intuition, the intuitions are contested, and there is no agreed procedure for settling conflicts among them, which is Wainwright's and van Inwagen's objection and it is a good one. A method that yields a coherent description because its practitioners keep adjusting the inputs has not shown the description is satisfied. It has shown only that consistency is achievable, which was worth establishing, since the incompatibility arguments claimed it was not.
</Reply>
