The previous article closed with a question it did not answer. If a belief has a comforting cause, and the inference from a belief's origin to its falsity is a recognised mistake, what is the point of explaining where religion comes from?

There is a point, and locating it precisely is most of the work. The explanations on offer have improved enormously in the last thirty years, and what they establish is narrower than their popular use suggests and considerably more than nothing.

## The discipline

<Definition term="Genetic fallacy">
The mistake of inferring that a claim is true or false from facts about how the claim came to be believed or proposed.
</Definition>

The reason it is a mistake is that causes and truth-values are independent. Someone who believes there is a snake in the grass because they are terrified of snakes may be right about the snake. Kekulé's account of arriving at the ring structure of benzene involves a daydream, and the structure is what it is. A belief acquired for terrible reasons can be true, and a belief acquired for excellent reasons can be false.

Now the qualification that the rest of this article depends on, and it is the reason the fallacy is not the end of the discussion.

An account of a belief's origin cannot show the belief false. It can bear on whether the believer is justified in holding it. Those are different properties, and confusing them is what generates the impression that any appeal to causes is fallacious.

An illustration makes the difference clear. Suppose you find that a friend's confident view about a company was implanted by a drug rather than by evidence. You have learned nothing about the company. You have learned a great deal about whether your friend's confidence is worth anything, and if you were relying on their confidence as your reason, you have lost your reason.

So the useful question about the material below is never whether it shows religious belief false, because it does not and its practitioners generally say so. It is whether it removes a support that the belief was resting on.

## The nineteenth-century accounts

<Definition term="Projection theory">
The view that religious belief arises by taking human properties, needs, or social facts and projecting them outward as an independent divine being.
</Definition>

Ludwig Feuerbach set out the version everything else descends from, in *Das Wesen des Christentums* (1841), translated by Marian Evans under the name George Eliot as *The Essence of Christianity* in 1854. Feuerbach's claim is that the predicates ascribed to God are human predicates, taken to their limit and placed outside their owner. Knowledge, power, and love are ours; the idea of God is the idea of those raised to infinity and made into a separate person, and worship is therefore the species contemplating its own nature and not recognising it.

Feuerbach did not present this as a demolition, which is worth knowing since he is usually cited as one. He took himself to be preserving the content of religion while relocating it, so that what had been said about God was retained as true about humanity.

Two developments followed and moved the mechanism to different places.

Freud, in *The Future of an Illusion* (1927), located it in the individual psyche and in the residue of childhood dependence on a protecting father, calling religious beliefs illusions in a technical sense, meaning beliefs whose adoption is driven by wish rather than by evidence, without prejudging whether they are also false.

Durkheim, in *Les formes élémentaires de la vie religieuse* (1912), located it in society. What is worshipped, on his account, is the collective itself, which really does exceed each individual, really does impose obligations, and really does outlast them, and the sacred is what society has set apart.

These accounts are important historically and they have a common weakness. They were arrived at by reflection rather than by investigation, they specify mechanisms nobody had tested, and in the form their authors gave them they are hard to falsify, since any religious phenomenon can be redescribed as projection after the fact.

Where they did make a prediction, the record is mixed rather than favourable. Each implies that religion should recede as education spreads, insecurity falls, and social forms change. In much of western Europe that has happened. Globally it has not, and the association between religiosity and insecurity holds well enough to keep the general idea alive while falling well short of what these theories claimed.

## The empirical successor

<Definition term="Cognitive science of religion">
The research programme explaining religious belief as a by-product of ordinary cognitive systems that evolved for other purposes, rather than as an adaptation for religion or as a product of reasoning.
</Definition>

The central move is the refusal to treat religion as a thing needing its own explanation. On this approach there is no religion module and no religious instinct. There are ordinary capacities, developed for detecting agents, attributing mental states, inferring purposes, and remembering and transmitting information, and religious concepts are what those capacities produce when they operate in the way they normally do.

Stewart Guthrie's *Faces in the Clouds* (1993) set out the anthropomorphism version: we take the human case as a default model and read humanness into things that do not have it, because that model is the one we know best and the one that matters most.

Pascal Boyer's *Religion Explained* (2001) contributed the transmission side. Concepts that violate one or two of our expectations about the kind of thing they are, while behaving normally in every other respect, are remembered and passed on much better than concepts that violate nothing and better than concepts that violate everything. A statue that hears prayers is memorable. A statue that does nothing is forgettable, and a statue that exists on Tuesdays, has no location, and is made of grief is not transmissible at all. Religious concepts cluster tightly in the narrow band that is optimally memorable, and this is a claim about what survives cultural transmission rather than about what anybody chose to believe.

Related findings concern children: a readiness to attribute purposes to natural objects, and intuitions about minds and bodies that make the idea of a mind persisting without a body easy to entertain. Whether these amount to what has been called an intuitive dualism, and how much they explain, is disputed.

<Definition term="Hyperactive agency detection">
The proposed cognitive system that infers the presence of an agent from minimal cues, and that is biased toward over-detection because the costs of the two possible errors are unequal.
</Definition>

Justin Barrett named the device and developed it, later preferring "hypersensitive" to "hyperactive" to cover a wider range of triggers, in work including *Why Would Anyone Believe in God?* (2004).

The argument for the bias is about asymmetric costs. Treating the wind in the grass as a predator wastes a little energy. Treating a predator as the wind ends the lineage. Under that asymmetry, selection favours a system that fires readily and is wrong most of the time, and the proposal is that a system tuned that way will also produce agents where there are none: in the storm, in the illness, in the run of misfortune.

Honesty about the state of the field is required here, because the popular presentations are more confident than the researchers. The cognitive science of religion is roughly three decades old. Agency detection in particular has faced empirical challenge, with experimental work questioning whether threatening conditions produce the predicted over-detection and whether the device does the explanatory work assigned to it. The by-product framework is well motivated and parts of it are well supported; the specific mechanisms are live hypotheses rather than results, and anyone using them in an argument should say so.

## What follows

<Definition term="Debunking argument">
An argument that a belief is unjustified because the best explanation of why people hold it makes no reference to the belief's being true.
</Definition>

The structure avoids the genetic fallacy by targeting justification rather than truth, as the first section set out.

Sharon Street gave the model case for morality in "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value", *Philosophical Studies* 127 (2006), pp. 109-166, arguing that if our evaluative attitudes were shaped by selection, and selection tracks reproductive advantage rather than moral truth, then the realist owes an account of why the two should have coincided, and has no good one available.

Applied here, the argument runs: religious belief is produced by mechanisms whose operation is fully explained without reference to any god; a mechanism that would produce the belief whether or not the belief were true provides no evidence for it; so belief formed that way is unsupported.

Two conditions have to hold for this to work, and both are contestable.

The mechanism has to be insensitive to the truth of what it produces. This is the strong step. A mechanism can be caused, evolved, and reliable all at once: vision is a product of selection, has a complete natural explanation, and tracks the world, which is why the account of its origin does not debunk what we see.

And there has to be no independent route to the belief. A debunking explanation of how someone first came to a view does not touch the view if they now hold it for reasons that stand on their own. This is the limit that matters most for this subject, since the eleven preceding articles consisted entirely of such reasons. The debunker's answer is that the arguments were built to support a conclusion already held, which is the objection from the theodicies article reappearing, and it is answered the same way: by examining the arguments.

## The replies

Barrett's own response is the one to take seriously, partly because he is a practitioner rather than a critic of the field. If God intended people to be capable of recognising divine action, then equipping them with a strong disposition to detect agency is a reasonable way to have done it, and finding the mechanism no more debunks its output than finding the visual system debunks sight. A cause and a reason are not competitors.

The limit of this reply should be stated with the reply. It is available only to someone who already holds that there is a God who designed the mechanism. It defends theism against the debunking argument; it does not offer any support for theism, and using the discovery of the mechanism as evidence for design would be helping oneself to the conclusion.

The second reply is that the argument generalises past where its users want to stop. The same structure applies to moral beliefs, which Street pressed deliberately, and can be extended to mathematical beliefs and to beliefs about the external world, none of which selection shaped for the purpose of getting things right. A debunker who wants to keep those and lose religion owes an account of the difference. There is a good answer available, which is that we have independent checks on the deliverances of vision and arithmetic that we do not have on reports about gods, and that answer is the interesting reply rather than a dismissal of the generalisation.

The third reply holds that religious belief can be justified without being based on arguments or on the deliverances of a mechanism, which changes the question rather than answering it. That is a substantial position with a substantial literature, and the advanced tier takes it up.

<Aside title="What the programme actually accomplishes">
Something real is taken away by this material, and it is worth naming, because both sides tend to overstate.

For a long time the near-universality of religious belief was itself offered as evidence, on the ground that a conviction found in every society is unlikely to be a mistake. If there is a good explanation of why every society would produce such beliefs whether or not anything answered to them, that argument is finished. This is the clearest result in the area and it is not small.

The programme also removes a certain kind of puzzlement that did evidential work informally. Religious belief no longer needs a special explanation, and neither does its persistence, so nothing about its existence stands as a datum requiring a divine cause.

What it does not do is establish anything about whether there is a God, and the researchers say so. The symmetry is worth noticing too. There are natural explanations available for unbelief as well, involving education, security, and social environment, and they are equally beside the point. If a causal story about how a belief was acquired settled its truth, it would settle it in both directions at once, which is a reason for thinking it settles it in neither.
</Aside>

<Objection>
The reply about reliable mechanisms proves too little, and the case for that is in the output.

A visual system is defended by its record: independent observers converge, predictions succeed, and errors are correctable by further looking. The agency-detecting system has produced thousands of mutually incompatible accounts of what the agents are, what they want, and what they did, with no procedure anywhere for adjudicating between them, and no case on record of a religious question being settled by further investigation.

That is not an argument from origins at all. It is an argument from performance, and a faculty whose outputs contradict each other everywhere and cannot be checked anywhere is not a faculty anyone should trust, whatever its provenance.
</Objection>

<Reply>
This is the strongest form of the argument and it is worth separating from the weaker one it usually travels with, since the point being made has almost nothing to do with cognitive science. It is the conflicting claims objection from the article on religious experience, now aimed at the mechanism rather than at the experiences.

That relocation is a genuine improvement, and the comparison with vision is fair on its own terms. Convergence and correctability are what license trust in a faculty, and they are exactly what is missing.

Two qualifications are owed, and neither rescues the position being attacked.

The first is that the disagreement is less total than the sharpest version needs. Across traditions with no contact, there is convergence on there being something beyond the ordinary order that makes demands on people, and divergence on nearly everything after that. A defender can argue the convergence tracks something and the divergence is cultural elaboration. That was available in the earlier article, at the price of a much thinner conclusion than any tradition wants, and it is available here at the same price.

The second is that the demand for a procedure is not quite the demand for correctability. The traditions do have internal procedures for settling disputes, and they have settled some. What they lack is a procedure that works across traditions, which is what the objection needs and is a real absence.

The honest summary of the tier's last question is this. The natural explanations do not show that theism is false, they do dispose of the argument from universal belief, and they shift the burden onto the arguments considered in the preceding eleven articles rather than onto anything about how belief arises. Which is roughly where the subject was before, with one fewer support and a much better account of why the question keeps being asked.
</Reply>
