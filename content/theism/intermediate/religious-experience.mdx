The four arguments so far have been public. Their premises are available to anyone: that a universe exists, that its constants fall in a narrow band, that a concept can be formed, that obligations bind. Anyone can inspect the premises and judge the inference.

This one is not like that, and it is also, by a wide margin, the reason most religious people actually give when asked. Very few believers arrived at their position by weighing cosmological arguments. A great many report something they take to have happened to them.

Whether that has any evidential standing for the person it happened to, and whether it has any for anyone else, is the subject of this article. Both questions have answers that are more interesting than the confident positions on either side suggest.

## What counts

<Definition term="Religious experience">
An experience that seems to the person having it to be of some objective reality, and that has religious significance either in its content or in the interpretation the person places on it.
</Definition>

Two components are doing work in that definition, and both exclude things that are often confused with the subject.

The experience has to seem to be *of* something. Being moved by a hymn is a religious feeling and it is not a religious experience in this sense, because nothing presents itself as encountered. The same goes for a general sense of wonder at the scale of the universe, unless it comes with the impression of something's presence.

And the seeming has to be of something objective, meaning something that the subject takes to exist independently of the experiencing. A person who reports a vivid feeling and describes it as their own emotional state has not reported a religious experience in this sense either.

Within those bounds the range is very wide. At one end are the dramatic cases: visions, voices, conversions that reverse a life in an afternoon. At the other, and far more common, is a quiet and persistent sense of being accompanied, reported by people who have nothing dramatic to describe and who would not have thought to mention it if asked about anything else.

For the argument, the quiet end matters more than the dramatic end. Dramatic cases are the easiest to explain away, because they cluster in conditions that have obvious alternative explanations, and there are few of them. The undramatic cases are numerous, distributed across every society anyone has studied, and reported by people who are in no way unusual.

The argument built on this material is not a demonstration and nobody presents it as one. It has the probabilistic shape set out in the beginner tier: the question is whether the existence and character of these experiences is more to be expected if theism is true than if it is false, and by how much.

## Two kinds of report

The literature separates two things that popular discussion runs together, and the distinction turns out to matter for the hardest objection later on.

<Definition term="Mystical experience">
An experience characterised by a sense of unity, understood either as unity perceived in and through the world of ordinary objects or as unity found by emptying the mind of ordinary content, and typically reported as beyond adequate expression while carrying a conviction of insight.
</Definition>

William James gave the description that is still the starting point, in *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, published in 1902 and delivered as the Gifford Lectures at Edinburgh in the preceding two years. He identified four marks.

Ineffability: the subject reports that no adequate account of the content can be given in words, which is not a report of difficulty but a claim about the kind of thing it was.

Noetic quality: despite resembling states of feeling, these states present themselves to the subject as states of knowledge, carrying insight and an authority that persists afterwards.

Transiency: they do not last, and cannot be sustained by trying.

Passivity: the subject feels acted upon rather than acting, as though the state arrived rather than was achieved, even when practices were used to prepare for it.

The first two marks are an awkward pair, and the awkwardness is the whole epistemological problem in miniature. The experience presents itself as delivering knowledge while resisting any statement of what is known. Ordinary knowledge can be handed on, checked, and corrected. Something that arrives with conviction and cannot be put into a form anyone else can assess is hard to place in an evidential scheme, and this is true whether or not it is veridical.

Walter Stace, in *Mysticism and Philosophy* of 1960, divided these experiences into two types. In extrovertive mysticism the unity is perceived through the senses, in and behind the ordinary world of objects. In introvertive mysticism it is reached by emptying consciousness of content altogether. Stace held that both culminate in an apprehension of unity, and that claim about a shared core is precisely what the objections below attack.

<Definition term="The numinous">
The distinctive object of religious dread and attraction, described by Rudolf Otto as an overwhelming and wholly other mystery that at once terrifies and compels, and treated by him as a category that cannot be reduced to moral or rational terms.
</Definition>

Rudolf Otto coined the word from the Latin *numen* in *Das Heilige*, published in 1917 and translated into English as *The Idea of the Holy* in 1923. His formula for the object is *mysterium tremendum et fascinans*: a mystery that is overwhelming and awful in the older sense of the word, and simultaneously fascinating in a way that draws the subject toward what is terrifying them.

Otto's claim was that this response is not a form of belief, not a moral response, and not a rational judgement, and that attempts to analyse it into those things lose what is characteristic about it. Whatever one makes of that, the category earns its place here for a structural reason.

Numinous experience and mystical union are not the same thing and they pull in opposite directions. The numinous is confrontation with something radically other, and the distance between subject and object is the heart of it. Mystical union is the dissolving of exactly that distance. Traditions differ over which they treat as the higher state, and any argument that pools religious experiences into a single body of evidence has to reckon with the fact that its data include reports of a personal other and reports of the disappearance of the personal altogether.

## Swinburne's two principles

The argument that religious experience is evidence was given its most careful statement by Richard Swinburne in *The Existence of God*, published by Oxford University Press in 1979 with a second edition in 2004. It runs on two principles, neither of which is invented for the occasion.

<Definition term="Principle of credulity">
The principle that if it seems to a person that something is present, then probably it is present, in the absence of special considerations that would override the appearance.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Principle of testimony">
The principle that, in the absence of special considerations, the reports people give of their experiences are probably accurate accounts of how things seemed to them.
</Definition>

The case for the first principle does not depend on anything religious. Without something like it, ordinary perception collapses, because there is no non-circular way to establish that perception is reliable. You cannot check whether your eyes work without using something, and whatever you use is itself a faculty whose reliability was in question. Every workable epistemology grants perception a default entitlement and then allows it to be overridden case by case, and the principle of credulity is a statement of that policy.

The second is in the same position. Nearly everything anyone knows about history, geography, other countries, or science they have not personally done rests on taking other people's reports as probably accurate. A policy of believing testimony only when independently confirmed would leave almost nothing standing.

Swinburne's argument is then short. Religious experiences are experiences. The principles apply to experiences in general. So they apply to these unless a principled reason is produced for excluding them, and the burden of producing it falls on whoever wants the exclusion. What the burden cannot be discharged by is a general reluctance about the conclusion, since that is not a criterion.

The principles are defeasible by design, and Swinburne allows several kinds of consideration to override them: doubts about the subject's reliability or state, doubts about the conditions, background evidence making the object's existence improbable, and grounds for thinking the putative object was not what produced the experience. The dispute is over whether religious cases are systematically vulnerable to these in a way ordinary perception is not.

## The objections

### Nothing checks it

The first objection is that religious experience lacks everything that makes perception trustworthy. Other people can look at the tree and report the same tree. Perceptual claims generate expectations that can be disappointed. Errors are detectable and get detected. None of this holds for a reported encounter with God, where there is no second observer, no prediction, and no procedure that would reveal a mistake.

William Alston's reply, in *Perceiving God*, published by Cornell University Press in 1991, is that the demand smuggles in the standards of one practice and applies them to another. His framework treats sense perception, memory, introspection, and reasoning as doxastic practices: settled ways of forming beliefs, each with its own characteristic checks, none of which can be justified without relying on itself. Sense perception cannot pass the test the objection sets, because any attempt to validate it uses it. If circular support and internal checks are enough for those practices, then a religious practice conducted within a tradition, which does have internal checks in the form of doctrinal constraints and the accumulated judgements of that tradition about which experiences to trust, is in a comparable position.

The result should be stated with the modesty Alston himself gave it. This is a defensive conclusion. It shows that the practice has not been convicted of irrationality by a standard that its critics can consistently apply, and it does not show that the practice delivers truth. Alston knew the difference and said so.

### It has a natural cause

The second objection observes that these experiences have neural correlates, that some of them can be induced, and that certain physiological states produce them with some reliability.

The general form of this objection is weak, and the weakness should be stated before the strong version, because the general form is what usually gets asserted. Every veridical perception has a neural correlate. Finding the mechanism by which an experience occurs does not show that the mechanism is not a means of detecting something, any more than describing the optics of the eye shows that nobody sees trees. Identifying a cause and identifying a debunking cause are different achievements.

The strong version is a real difficulty. What undermines an experience is a cause that would have produced it whether or not the object was there. If a particular kind of experience arises reliably from sleep deprivation, or from a specific temporal lobe event, and would have arisen in exactly that form in a world with no God, then that particular experience carries no weight. For some reported cases this is plainly the situation, and the argument's defenders concede those cases and rest the argument on the rest.

### The concepts were brought to it

Steven Katz argued in "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism", published in the 1978 collection *Mysticism and Philosophical Analysis* by Oxford University Press, that there are no unmediated experiences of any kind, and that mystical experience is shaped throughout by the concepts, expectations, and training the mystic brings. Christians have Christian experiences and Buddhists have Buddhist ones, and this is not a coincidence to be explained by a shared object described differently. It is what the constructive role of the subject's framework predicts.

The reply available to Swinburne and Alston is that mediation is universal and does not by itself debunk. An ornithologist and a novice looking at the same bird have different experiences, structured by different concepts, and the ornithologist's experience is more informative rather than less veridical. Concept-laden perception is still perception.

Where Katz's argument does damage is to a particular use of the evidence. The older versions of the argument leaned on cross-cultural convergence: the claim that mystics everywhere report the same thing, which would be difficult to explain without a common object. If the convergence is an artefact of describing the reports at a level of abstraction that removes everything the traditions actually say, then the convergence is produced by the analyst rather than found in the data. That was the strongest evidential feature of the argument as Stace presented it, and it has not recovered.

## The diversity problem

<Definition term="Conflicting claims objection">
The argument that since religious experiences across different traditions support incompatible claims about what is experienced, they cannot all be veridical, and no way of privileging one tradition's experiences is available that does not assume what is in question.
</Definition>

This is a sharper objection than the observation that religious people disagree, and the sharpening is worth setting out.

The disagreement is not confined to the doctrine built on top of the experiences. It reaches into the reported content. Some experiences are of a personal being who addresses the subject. Some are of an impersonal absolute in which distinctions dissolve. Some are of the absence of any enduring self at all, which is what a Buddhist practitioner may report and take to confirm a doctrine incompatible with theism. Some are of many divine beings. These reports cannot all be accurate about their object, and the argument from religious experience proposes to treat all of them as evidence under the same principle.

Three responses are on offer and each has an identifiable cost.

John Hick proposed that the traditions are responses to a single reality encountered under the categories each brings, so that no tradition describes that reality as it is in itself. The experiences are then all veridical in the way perception of a single object through different instruments is veridical. The cost is that the reality as it is in itself becomes something none of the traditions is actually about, and most practitioners reject that as a description of what they take themselves to be doing. The advanced tier takes up this position and its rivals directly.

Alston and Alvin Plantinga argued instead that a person inside a tradition has epistemic resources unavailable from outside it, and remains rational in continuing to rely on that tradition's practice even while recognising that someone in another tradition is symmetrically placed. The cost is that the symmetry is conceded and nothing adjudicates it. Alston regarded this as an uncomfortable result rather than a solution, and said so, which is more than the position's later users have generally done.

The third response is deflationary. The conflicts are sharpest at the level of interpretation and doctrine, and thinnest at the level of what is experienced, so what survives as common evidence is something like this: something transcendent, of overwhelming value, encountered rather than constructed. That is defensible, and after Katz it is the only version of the perennialist claim still standing. Its cost is that what survives may be too thin to be worth the argument. A transcendent something of overwhelming value is compatible with an impersonal absolute, and the argument was supposed to support a personal God.

<Aside title="Two different questions about the same experience">
The argument has a structure that is easy to miss, and missing it produces a great deal of pointless argument between believers and their critics.

For the person who had the experience, it functions as a perception, and the principle of credulity applies at full strength. Someone who takes themselves to have been addressed is in the position of someone who takes themselves to have seen a friend across a street: entitled to believe it, and not obliged to construct an argument first.

For everyone else, it arrives as testimony, and the principle of testimony applies. That gives it weight, and it does not give it the weight it has for the subject. This is not a special disability of religious experience. A friend's report that they saw a rare bird is evidence, and it is weaker evidence than their own seeing was for them, and everyone accepts this in ordinary life without treating it as scepticism.

So the believer who cannot understand why others are unmoved, and the critic who cannot understand why the believer will not treat the matter as open, are both making the same mistake in opposite directions. They are assuming that one body of evidence must have the same force from every position.
</Aside>

<Objection>
Look at how these experiences are distributed. Everything about the pattern is what you would predict if the mind were producing them and nothing about it is what you would predict if something were being encountered.

People experience the God of the culture that raised them, in the vocabulary that culture supplied, confirming the doctrines that culture taught. Frequency tracks expectation and practice: those who seek such experiences within a tradition that promises them find them, and those who do not, do not.

And in the whole recorded body of these reports, across every tradition, no one has ever come back with information they could not have had. Nobody has returned from an encounter with the divine knowing a fact about the world that was later confirmed and could not have been guessed. A genuine channel to an omniscient source would leak, and this one never has.

The content is also thin in exactly the place it would need to be thick. What comes back is a sense of presence, of love, of unity, of being known. What does not come back is anything that would settle any of the questions the traditions disagree about, which is precisely what an actual encounter would be positioned to settle.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The point about information is the strongest of these and it should be conceded in full. No verified case exists, the check has never been passed, and defenders of the argument who change the subject when it is raised are avoiding their best test rather than answering it. Whether an encounter of the kind reported would be expected to transmit propositional information is arguable, since being addressed is not the same as being briefed, and the argument here has not been won by either side.

The cultural point proves less than it appears to. Perception everywhere is shaped by training and by the concepts available, as the reply to Katz set out, and someone raised in one vocabulary would be expected to describe an encounter in that vocabulary whether or not there was something to describe. What the cultural point does establish is that the content of the experiences cannot be used as independent confirmation of any tradition's specific doctrines, which is a real restriction and one that Swinburne's version does not need.

The frequency point is a genuine difficulty and it is not neutralised by anything above. If the experiences occur when and where they are expected, and in proportion to how much they are sought, that is the profile of a produced state.

What the argument establishes, honestly stated, is considerably less than its enthusiasts claim and more than its critics allow. It does not establish theism, and no version of it defended by anyone competent claims to. What it establishes is that a very large body of reports exists, that the principles required to dismiss it wholesale would also dismiss ordinary perception and testimony, and that anyone assembling a case for or against theism has to assign these reports some weight rather than none. That is a modest result and it is the result that survives the objections.
</Reply>
