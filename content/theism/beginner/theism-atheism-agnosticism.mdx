Two people call themselves atheists. The first holds that the claim that God exists is false, and can say why. The second holds nothing at all on the matter and simply declines to believe, on the ground that no adequate case has been made.

When a theist argues with them, only the first is in the argument. The second has taken up a position that cannot be refuted, because it does not assert anything. Whether that is intellectual hygiene or an evasion is one of the live questions in this article, and settling it requires being clear about what these three words pick out.

## Positions as claims

<Definition term="Theism">
The proposition that God exists, where God is understood as in classical theism: one personal being, responsible for everything else, possessing maximal power, maximal knowledge, and perfect goodness.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Atheism">
The proposition that God does not exist: the denial of theism.
</Definition>

Notice what these definitions are definitions of. They pick out propositions, which are the sort of thing that can be true or false, argued for, and used as premises. They do not pick out states of a person.

That choice is deliberate and it is the standard one in academic philosophy of religion, for reasons worth stating rather than assuming.

It keeps the two positions symmetrical. Theism is almost never defined as a psychological condition, and defining its opposite as one produces a pair that cannot be compared.

It matches what the disputes are actually about. When someone argues that suffering counts against God's existence, the conclusion of that argument is a proposition, not a report of the arguer's state of mind.

And it keeps the words usable in arguments. A psychological state cannot be a premise or a conclusion, cannot be true, and cannot be supported by evidence. If atheism is defined as the absence of a belief, then no argument for atheism is possible even in principle, which is a strange result for a position with a large literature arguing for it.

Nothing in this rules out the other usage. It means that when the other usage is intended, this tier will say so, using different words.

## Positions as states of a person

<Definition term="Weak atheism">
The condition of a person who does not believe that God exists, without thereby believing that God does not exist. Also called negative atheism.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Strong atheism">
The condition of a person who believes that God does not exist. Also called positive atheism.
</Definition>

The pair comes from Michael Martin, whose *Atheism: A Philosophical Justification* of 1990 organises its case around the distinction, and the underlying idea is older than the labels. Antony Flew defended defining atheism as the mere absence of theistic belief in the early 1970s, and his reason for preferring that definition was an argument about who is obliged to make a case, which the last article in this tier takes up in full.

The distinction is real and it matters. Not believing that something is so and believing that it is not so are different conditions, and a great deal of ordinary reasoning depends on keeping them apart. A jury that returns a verdict of not guilty has not found the defendant innocent; it has found that the case was not made. Someone who has never heard of a proposition does not believe it, and does not thereby believe its negation.

<Aside title="Two ways of not believing">
Write the claim as a single proposition: God exists.

There are three attitudes a person can take to it. They can believe it. They can believe its negation. Or they can do neither, which includes never having considered it, having considered it and suspended judgement, and having considered it and found the question unanswerable.

The first attitude corresponds to theism as a psychological state. The second corresponds to strong atheism. Weak atheism, defined as the absence of the first attitude, covers the second and the third together, which is why it is a wider category than it usually sounds when someone claims it.

Two consequences follow and both are routinely missed.

A strong atheist is also a weak atheist, since believing that God does not exist entails not believing that God does. Someone who says "I am not a strong atheist, only a weak one" is saying something about which of the narrower categories they are in, and the wider label does not by itself convey it.

And a rock is a weak atheist, along with an infant and a person who has never encountered the question. That is not a joke at anyone's expense. It shows that the category is defined by an absence, and that being in it is not by itself evidence of anything, including of having reasoned well.
</Aside>

## Suspending judgement, and saying nobody can know

<Definition term="Agnosticism">
Either the condition of a person who suspends judgement about whether God exists, or the claim that nobody is justified in believing either that God exists or that God does not.
</Definition>

The two readings are packed into one word and separating them prevents most of the confusion around it.

On the first reading, agnosticism is a report. Someone describes themselves as neither believing nor disbelieving. Nothing has been claimed about what anyone else should think, or about the evidence, and there is nothing to argue with.

On the second reading, agnosticism is a substantive epistemological thesis. It says that the evidence does not support either position, and therefore that theists and atheists alike hold their views without adequate warrant. This is a strong claim about the state of the arguments, it is precisely as much in need of defence as theism or atheism, and it can be wrong.

Thomas Henry Huxley coined the word in 1869, at a meeting of the Metaphysical Society in London where every other member had a label to hand and he found he had none. His formation was built against the gnostic of church history, who claimed knowledge of exactly the matters Huxley took himself to be ignorant of. What he intended was closer to the second reading than the first: not a mood, but a rule about not professing certainty beyond the evidence.

The overlap with the previous section is the source of most of the arguing. Someone who suspends judgement is, on the psychological definitions, both an agnostic and a weak atheist. The two labels are not competing descriptions of the same person's beliefs; they are two different partitions of the same territory, and asking which one is correct is asking a question with no answer.

## One God or all of them

The definitions above were stated for the God of classical theism, and the restriction is not a technicality.

Denying that one specific being exists is a much smaller undertaking than denying that anything anyone has ever meant by a god exists. The arguments that get made in this subject are almost all directed at the first. An argument from the distribution of suffering, for instance, is aimed at a being who is at once all-powerful and perfectly good, and it has no force at all against a limited deity, an indifferent one, or a non-personal ultimate of the kind found in several major traditions.

This cuts against overreach on both sides. Someone who has answered the arguments for the God of classical theism has not shown that no such being exists. Someone who has refuted classical theism has not shown that every conception of the divine is empty, and would need either to work through the alternatives or to defend a general account of what the world contains, which is a much larger project.

The honest form of most positions in this subject is therefore narrower than the label suggests, and saying which conception is in view is part of stating the position at all.

## Who has to argue

<Definition term="Burden of proof">
The obligation, in a dispute, to make the case for one's position, borne by whichever party would lose if no argument at all were offered.
</Definition>

This is where the definitional work pays off, because how the words are defined determines who is obliged to do what.

If atheism is the proposition that God does not exist, then it is a claim about how things are, and the person asserting it owes reasons in the way the theist does. Two parties assert incompatible propositions, and neither gets a discount.

If atheism is the absence of belief, the shape changes. The atheist has asserted nothing, so there is nothing for them to defend, and the entire burden falls on the theist. Failure of the theistic arguments is then sufficient for the atheist's position to stand, without any positive case being made at all.

That is why the definitional dispute is not merely verbal. It settles in advance which side loses a stalemate.

Three cautions apply to any use of the notion.

The courtroom analogy is imperfect. A trial has to end in a verdict, so a default is institutionally required, and the presumption of innocence is a rule about what to do when the evidence is inconclusive rather than a claim about what is probable. Inquiry has no such requirement. Nothing forces a conclusion at the end of an inconclusive investigation, and suspending judgement is always available.

Assigning the burden to the party making the positive claim sounds neutral and is not. Both theism and its denial can be stated either positively or negatively depending on how the sentence is arranged, and any rule sensitive to that is a rule about grammar.

And the notion can be used to avoid the work. A party who never states a position, never defends one, and simply requires the other side to satisfy an unspecified standard has not participated in an inquiry. The reasonable version of the appeal is a claim about prior plausibility, namely that one hypothesis starts out likelier than the other and that inconclusive evidence therefore leaves it ahead. That is a real argument with a real premise, and it is where the last article in this tier picks the question up.

<Objection>
This is bookkeeping about words, and nothing about the world turns on it.

Whether a particular person is described as a weak atheist or as an agnostic is a fact about English usage. The person's actual epistemic situation, which is what matters, is unchanged by the label. Two parties who agree completely about the state of the evidence, the strength of the arguments, and the probability of the hypothesis can still argue for an hour about which word applies to them, and at the end of the hour they know nothing they did not know before.

The bookkeeping is also, in practice, a tactic. Definitions in this area are not chosen neutrally. Each party proposes the definition that puts the obligation on the other side, and the ensuing dispute about words is a proxy for a dispute about who has to do the work, conducted in a way that lets each side treat its own convenience as a matter of correct usage. Dropping the vocabulary entirely and asking directly how likely the hypothesis is on the available evidence would lose nothing and would save the hour.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The last suggestion is right and it is the reason for the distinctions rather than an objection to them.

Asking directly how likely the hypothesis is on the evidence is exactly what should happen, and someone who can do that has no further need for the labels. The distinctions exist because that question is very often not asked, and the labels are what obscure it. Working out that a self-described atheist has made no claim, or that a self-described agnostic has made a strong one about the evidence, is what turns a dispute about words into the question the objection recommends.

On the charge that the definitions are chosen for advantage, the accusation is fair and the response is to make the choice explicit rather than to abandon the vocabulary. This tier uses the propositional definitions, and it does so on grounds that were stated openly and can be disputed: symmetry with theism, usability in arguments, and correspondence with what the literature actually argues about. Anyone who prefers the psychological definitions can have them, provided they accept the consequence, which is that no argument for atheism so defined is possible and the position cannot be supported, only left standing.

What the objection underrates is that the burden question is not a squabble over etiquette. Almost every inquiry ends inconclusively, and where the residual weight falls determines what a reasonable person believes at the end of it. A framework that hands that outcome to whoever phrased their position as a negation has a defect, and noticing the defect requires precisely the bookkeeping the objection wants to skip.

The concession is on scale. The distinctions in this article are worth an article and not worth a career, and the amount of public argument spent on which label applies to whom is out of all proportion to what the labels settle.
</Reply>
