A hospital has one place left on a training programme and four applicants.

The first has the highest examination results by a clear margin. The second has worked for six years in difficult conditions to get here while the first was supported throughout. The third comes from the region the programme was set up to serve, where there are almost no doctors. The fourth was wrongly rejected last year through an administrative error that has since been admitted.

Each has a claim, each claim is of a different kind, and no amount of care about the facts will make three of them go away. The previous article said that every serious dispute about equality is a dispute about which differences between people are relevant. This one sets out the candidates.

## Three bases for a share

<Definition term="Desert">
What a person is owed in virtue of something they have done or some way they have been: their effort, their contribution, their conduct, or their character.
</Definition>

Desert looks backward at the person and asks what they have made of themselves. It is the basis behind the thought that people who work harder should get more, that a prize should go to the best performance, and that a wrongdoer should suffer for the wrong. The intuition is powerful and nearly universal, and it is also the most philosophically embattled of the three, for reasons the aside below sets out.

<Definition term="Merit">
Fitness for a particular position or task: the qualities, skills, and capacities that make someone able to do well what the position requires.
</Definition>

Merit is regularly conflated with desert and the two come apart sharply. Merit is forward-looking and role-relative. It asks who will do the job well, not who has earned the chance, and the answer can be someone who did nothing to acquire the relevant capacity. A person with an unusual natural aptitude has merit for a role without deserving anything, and a person who worked twice as hard to reach a lower standard may have more desert and less merit.

The distinction explains a familiar argument that otherwise looks like people talking past each other. Appointment on merit is defended as fair because the best-qualified candidate gets the post, and attacked as unfair because qualifications track advantages that the candidate did nothing to earn. Both claims can be true. Merit is the right criterion for allocating a role whose performance matters to third parties, and it was never a claim about what anyone had earned.

<Definition term="Need">
What a person requires in order to reach some threshold of functioning or well-being, independently of what they have done or what they can do.
</Definition>

Need looks at the person's condition rather than their record. It is the basis behind medical triage, disaster relief, and the provision of a minimum income, and it is the one basis on which the strongest claim can be held by the person who has contributed nothing.

The best-known statement of a needs principle is the formula popularised by Marx in the *Critique of the Gotha Programme*, written in 1875 and published by Engels in 1891: "from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs". Marx did not coin the phrase, which was already circulating in socialist writing, and he presented it as describing a condition that would become possible only after scarcity had been overcome rather than as a rule for allocating shares under present conditions.

Two difficulties attach to need wherever it is used. Needs must be distinguished from wants, and the line is genuinely hard to draw once anything beyond bare survival is included; a threshold set at subsistence is defensible and almost nobody thinks justice is satisfied by it. And a principle that responds only to need severs the connection between contribution and reward entirely, which the first three applicants above would have something to say about.

## Fair procedures and fair outcomes

Everything so far concerns what the distribution should look like. A different family of answers concerns how it came about.

<Definition term="Procedural justice">
Justice understood as a property of the process by which an outcome was reached, rather than of the outcome itself.
</Definition>

Rawls drew a three-way distinction in *A Theory of Justice* in 1971 that has become standard, and it is more useful than it first appears.

Perfect procedural justice applies when there is an independent standard for the right outcome and a procedure guaranteed to produce it. Dividing a cake equally is the illustration: let the person who cuts choose last, and the equal division follows.

Imperfect procedural justice applies when there is an independent standard for the right outcome but no procedure guaranteed to reach it. A criminal trial is the case. The right outcome is that the guilty are convicted and the innocent acquitted, this is fixed independently of what the trial produces, and no arrangement of rules and evidence reaches it every time. The best available procedure is followed and its verdicts are sometimes wrong.

Pure procedural justice applies when there is no independent standard at all, and the outcome is just because the procedure was. A lottery is the case. No particular winner was the right winner, and what makes the result fair is that the draw was fair.

The classification matters because a great many political arguments turn on which category a case falls into. If the distribution of income is a matter of pure procedural justice, then whatever emerges from fair transactions is just and there is nothing further to assess. If it is imperfect procedural justice, then there is a standard the outcome can fail to meet, and market results are open to criticism no matter how the transactions were conducted. That disagreement is not about economics; it is about which kind of procedural justice is in play, and it is one of the deepest divisions in the subject.

<Definition term="Corrective justice">
Justice in the rectification of a wrong: restoring the injured party, so far as possible, to the position they would have occupied had the wrong not been done.
</Definition>

Aristotle separated this from distribution in Book V of the *Nicomachean Ethics* and the separation has held. Corrective justice is bilateral, holding between a wrongdoer and the person they wronged, and it takes no interest in how well off either of them is. A rich person who defrauds a poor one owes restitution, and a rich person who defrauds another rich person owes exactly as much. The measure is the wrong, not the resulting distribution.

The fourth applicant in the opening case is making a corrective claim and it should not be assessed alongside the others. Their argument is not that they need the place, or deserve it, or would do the job best. It is that a wrong was done to them and has not been undone, and the remedy is owed by whoever did it regardless of what the distributive picture looks like.

Whether corrective justice is genuinely independent of distributive justice, or whether restoring someone to a prior position presupposes that the prior position was itself just, is a serious question and the advanced tier takes it up.

## Formal and substantive

<Definition term="Substantive equality">
Equality in the actual condition or outcome of people's lives, as against equality only in the rules applied to them.
</Definition>

The previous article set out formal equality and showed that it is nearly empty on its own. Substantive equality is what the demand becomes when it is given content: not merely that the rule is the same for everyone, but that people end up standing in something like equal condition.

The gap between them is where most practical argument lives. A rule requiring every applicant to sit the same examination is formally equal and produces results that track years of unequal preparation. A rule setting the same minimum wage everywhere is formally equal and means something quite different in places where the cost of living differs by a factor of three. Formal equality is a constraint on arbitrariness, satisfied by any consistently applied rule, and consistency is compatible with almost any outcome.

Substantive equality is the more demanding notion and it inherits every difficulty from the "equality of what" question in the previous article. It also collides directly with desert. A distribution that responds to how hard people worked will not be substantively equal, since people do not work equally hard, and any principle that insists on equal outcomes has to say what happened to the desert claim.

<Aside title="The trouble with desert">
Desert is the criterion ordinary moral thought reaches for first and the one philosophers have found hardest to defend, and the argument against it is worth stating in full because its conclusion is genuinely unattractive.

Suppose someone deserves more because they worked harder. Working hard is largely a matter of conscientiousness, energy, and the capacity for sustained attention. These are shaped by upbringing, by neurology, and by circumstances nobody chose. Push the question back a step and the capacity to make the effort turns out to be as unearned as the talent that the effort was supposed to distinguish itself from.

So the argument runs. What a person contributes depends on their talents, which they did not earn. What they achieve with those talents depends on their effort, which depends on a character they did not choose and circumstances they did not arrange. Nothing is left over that could be the thing they earned, and desert has no foundation.

Three responses are on the table. The first accepts the argument and drops desert, keeping merit for allocating roles and need for allocating resources, and treating rewards as incentives rather than as anything anyone is owed. The second holds that the argument proves too much, since the same reasoning would abolish responsibility altogether and with it praise, blame, gratitude, and the criminal law, and that a conclusion that extreme is better treated as a reductio of the premise. The third looks for a form of desert that does not require ultimate authorship, holding that what a person deserves depends on how they exercised the capacities they have rather than on whether they authored those capacities.

The dispute connects directly to the free will problem in metaphysics, and any view about desert in political philosophy is committing itself, whether it says so or not, to something about responsibility.
</Aside>

<Objection>
The whole apparatus assumes a distributor. Talk of dividing benefits and burdens according to a criterion pictures a pile of goods and someone deciding who gets what, and no such pile and no such person exist.

Goods come into the world already attached to people. Someone grew the crop, someone wrote the software, someone offered a service that others chose to pay for. The result is not a distribution that anyone made; it is the aggregate of countless transactions between people entitled to what they were transacting with. Asking whether that aggregate matches a pattern of desert or need is asking the wrong question, in the way that asking whether the pattern of who married whom is just would be the wrong question.

On this view the only justice at issue is in the transactions. If people acquired their holdings without wronging anyone and transferred them voluntarily, the outcome is just whatever it looks like, and any attempt to adjust it toward a preferred pattern means overriding choices that were nobody else's business.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The objection is the sharpest in the field and it changed what defenders of patterned principles have to argue. Two replies are standard and a third concession is honest.

The first reply denies the picture of goods arriving pre-attached. Almost nothing anyone produces is produced alone. It depends on a legal system that defines and enforces ownership, on infrastructure, on an educated workforce, on a currency, and on the accumulated knowledge of everyone who came before. What a person takes home is not a natural fact but the output of a system of rules that could have been drawn differently, and asking whether those rules are just is not a confused question.

The second reply attacks the starting point. The transactional account says a distribution is just if it descends from just holdings by just steps, and the actual holdings people started from descend from conquest, enslavement, expropriation, and fraud on a scale that no one disputes. A theory whose verdict depends on a clean history delivers no verdict at all about any real society, and its own logic then demands rectification of a magnitude its adherents rarely embrace.

The concession is that the objection is right about how the question is usually posed. Political philosophy does speak as though society were dividing a pile, and it inherits the habit from thought experiments in which a fixed quantity is allocated among people who did not produce it. Real economies are not like that: what is produced depends on the rules under which people are working, so a principle that ignores its own effects on production is not merely idealised but incoherent. Taking that seriously does not vindicate the transactional view. It means the distributive question has to be asked about the rules of the system rather than about a snapshot of who currently holds what, and later tiers show what a theory looks like when it is framed that way.
</Reply>

Three of the four applicants were pressing claims about what they should receive. The fourth was pressing something different: a claim that something was owed to them and withheld. That form of claim has a vocabulary of its own, and it has become the dominant one in political argument. The next article introduces it.
