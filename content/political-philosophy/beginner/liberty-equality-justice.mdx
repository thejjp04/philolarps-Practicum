A state requires everyone above a certain income to contribute a portion of it, and uses the proceeds to fund schooling for children whose families could not otherwise pay.

Three objections can be made to that arrangement and they have nothing to do with each other. The first is that people are being made to hand over money they earned, under threat, for purposes they did not choose. The second is that the scheme leaves untouched the far larger advantages that wealthy children have, so it does not achieve what it claims. The third is that the burden falls on the wrong people or that the benefit reaches the wrong ones.

These are complaints in the names of liberty, equality, and justice. Getting clear on what each is demanding, and on why satisfying one can mean failing another, is what this article does.

## Liberty in two senses

<Definition term="Negative liberty">
Freedom understood as the absence of interference: a person is free to the extent that no one else prevents them from doing what they might otherwise do.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Positive liberty">
Freedom understood as the presence of control: a person is free to the extent that they are the author of their own life, in command of themselves and able to act on their own purposes.
</Definition>

Isaiah Berlin drew the distinction in an essay of 1958 called "Two Concepts of Liberty", later collected in *Four Essays on Liberty* in 1969, and it has organised the discussion ever since.

The negative sense answers the question of how far someone is left alone. Its measure is the number and importance of the doors standing open, and it is silent on whether the person has any means of walking through them. A person with no money is not, on this account, made unfree by their poverty, since nobody is stopping them buying anything. Whether that is a virtue of the account or its central defect is one of the standing disputes in the subject.

The positive sense answers a different question, about who is in charge. Its natural examples are addiction, indoctrination, and desperation, where nobody is interfering and yet the person is not really running their own life. And it makes room for something the negative sense cannot express, namely that being governed by a body you have a share in is a kind of freedom even though you are subject to its rules.

Berlin's argument was that the second concept is dangerous, and the shape of the worry is worth setting out because it is often reduced to a slogan. If freedom is self-mastery, there must be a self that does the mastering and a self that gets mastered. Divide the person into a rational or higher self and a merely appetitive one, and it becomes possible to say that a person's real self wants something other than what they say they want. From there it is a short step to the claim that coercing someone can free them, since it aligns them with what they would have wanted had they been thinking clearly. Regimes that imprisoned people while describing it as liberation were not making an obvious verbal error, and Berlin's point was that the concept had been available to them.

## One concept or two

The distinction has been attacked at its foundation. Gerald MacCallum argued in 1967 that there is a single concept of freedom with three slots in it: an agent is free from some constraint to do or become something. Every intelligible claim about freedom fills all three, and what look like two concepts are disagreements about how to fill the slots.

The point is a good one and it does real work. Someone who says the poor are unfree and someone who says they are not are usually not disagreeing about the meaning of freedom. They are disagreeing about what counts as a constraint, which is a substantive dispute about which obstacles are the kind that matter, and stating it as a dispute over which concept is the real one obscures what is actually at issue.

What MacCallum's analysis does not do is remove the disagreement. It relocates it, which is progress, and the question of whether lacking the means to do something is a constraint on doing it remains exactly as difficult after the relocation as before.

## Equality and the emptiest true principle in philosophy

<Definition term="Equality">
The demand that people be treated as of equal standing, or that some benefit or burden be distributed evenly among them, depending on which respect is in question.
</Definition>

The definition has to be given in two parts because the word covers two things that come apart. Equal standing is a claim about how people are to be regarded: as equals, none of them counting for more than another when their interests are weighed. An even distribution is a claim about outcomes, and it is possible to insist on the first while denying that the second follows.

<Definition term="Formal equality">
The requirement that like cases be treated alike: that where two people do not differ in any relevant respect, they be treated the same.
</Definition>

The principle goes back to Aristotle's discussion of justice in Book V of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, and almost nobody denies it. It is also very nearly empty on its own, and understanding why is worth more than any number of arguments about it.

The principle says to treat like cases alike. It does not say which likenesses are relevant. A legal system that denies certain people the vote is not violating it, provided it denies the vote to all of them consistently, because on that system's account of relevance those people differ in a relevant respect. Every serious dispute about equality is a dispute about which differences count, and formal equality is silent on precisely that question. It rules out arbitrariness and caprice, which is not nothing, and it settles nothing anyone was arguing about.

That is why the interesting work happens one level down, in the arguments about which differences between people can justify treating them differently: effort, need, contribution, choice, birth, and so on. The next article is about those arguments.

<Aside title="Equality of what">
Someone who says people should be more equal has not yet said in what respect, and the candidates give very different answers.

Equality of resources gives everyone the same bundle of external means. It has the merit of being measurable and the defect of ignoring that people need different amounts to reach the same condition: a person with a disability requiring expensive equipment gets the same as everyone else and ends up worse off.

Equality of welfare fixes that by equalising how well people's lives go by their own lights. It has the defect of rewarding expensive taste, since a person who is only content with rare wines has a claim on more, and there is something wrong with a principle that says so.

Equality of opportunity equalises starting positions and lets outcomes fall where they will. Its difficulty is that starting positions include everything from schooling to the household someone grew up in to their inherited traits, and each attempt to specify how far back the equalisation reaches either stops arbitrarily or becomes extremely demanding.

Equality of standing abandons distribution and asks instead that no one stand in a relation of inferiority or subordination to anyone else. It captures something the other three miss, since a society could be materially equal and still deeply hierarchical, and it is correspondingly harder to say what it requires in practice.

Nothing here is settled, and the intermediate and advanced tiers work through these positions properly. The point for now is that "more equality" is not a determinate demand until the respect is specified, and that arguments in which the parties have specified it differently generate a great deal of heat and no progress.
</Aside>

## Justice as the third demand

<Definition term="Distributive justice">
The question of how the benefits and burdens of social cooperation ought to be allocated among the people who take part in it: who is entitled to what, and on what basis.
</Definition>

Justice is not a third name for equality. An equal distribution can be unjust, if some people worked and others did not and everyone receives the same. An unequal distribution can be just, if the inequality tracks something that matters. Egalitarian answers are one family of answers to the distributive question rather than the question itself, and treating the two as interchangeable makes the actual arguments impossible to state.

Nor is justice a third name for liberty, though one influential tradition comes close to saying so. On that view a distribution is just when it arose from voluntary transactions between people entitled to what they held, whatever the resulting pattern looks like. The advanced tier examines it. Notice only that it makes justice a matter of how a distribution came about rather than of what it looks like, and that the two tests can disagree about the same distribution.

## Where they collide

The three demands are not variations on one idea and they conflict in specific, identifiable ways.

Redistribution requires coercion. Money is not moved from one person to another by request, and any state that equalises anything is interfering with people who would rather it did not. Every egalitarian programme costs negative liberty, and the only question is how much and whether it is worth it.

Equality can be pursued at the expense of positive liberty as well, wherever a scheme decides on people's behalf what they need. A state that provides a uniform benefit in kind rather than money has equalised something at the cost of some people's control over their own lives, which is a real loss and not a rhetorical one.

Formal equality can entrench substantive inequality. A rule applied identically to everyone falls very differently on people whose situations differ, and a policy of perfect consistency is compatible with an outcome no one would defend.

And negative liberty is not distributed equally by leaving people alone. The person who owns land and the person who owns nothing both face no interference, and one of them has vastly more open doors. Non-interference is not the same as being equally free, and much of the argument between the political traditions turns on this observation and what to do about it.

<Objection>
The conflict is manufactured. It appears only because liberty has been defined as non-interference, and that definition was chosen by people who wanted a vocabulary in which taxation is oppression and destitution is not.

Take freedom to mean what a person is actually able to do, and the supposed tension dissolves. Redistribution does not reduce freedom; it moves freedom from someone who had a great deal to someone who had almost none, and increases the total. The person who can now afford to leave a job, move house, or refuse an offer has gained far more than the person taxed has lost. Presenting this as a trade-off between liberty and equality is a rhetorical achievement of one side of the argument, not a discovery about the structure of political value.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The observation is correct and the conclusion overreaches.

It is entirely true that the negative conception was not handed down and that adopting it loads the dice. A vocabulary in which a tax bill counts as an interference with freedom and having no options at all does not is a vocabulary that will keep reaching certain conclusions. Noticing that is a genuine contribution and it is why the resource-based conception has as many defenders as it does.

Two things stop it dissolving the conflict.

The first is that the objection needs freedom to be a quantity that can be summed across people, so that a gain to one can exceed a loss to another. That is a substantial assumption, it is not obviously coherent, and it makes freedom into something to be maximised in a way that the objection's own tradition usually resists when the same move is made with welfare.

The second is that a genuine loss to an identifiable person is not answered by an aggregate gain. The taxed person is in fact prevented, by threat, from doing what they would otherwise have done. Saying that freedom overall has increased does not make that untrue; it says the cost was worth paying, which is a different claim and one that has to be argued rather than defined into place.

The lasting point is on the objection's side even so. Whichever conception is adopted, it should be adopted with the consequences in view rather than absorbed as the neutral meaning of the word, and a great deal of political argument consists of two parties using the word in different senses and each finding the other unreasonable.
</Reply>
