"I have a right to be paid for the work I did." "I have a right to wear whatever I like."

Both sentences are ordinary English and they are not making the same kind of claim. The first says something about what someone else must do. The second says something about what nobody may stop you doing, and identifies no one who owes you anything. Rights talk runs the two together constantly, and a great deal of political argument gets nowhere because of it.

## Two things the word covers

Wesley Hohfeld, an American legal theorist, produced the analysis that sorted this out, in a paper in the *Yale Law Journal* in 1913. His subject was legal reasoning rather than political morality, and philosophers took the apparatus over because nothing better has been produced since.

<Definition term="Claim right">
A right that another party has a duty to you: you have a claim right that someone do or refrain from something exactly when they have a duty to you to do or refrain from it.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Liberty right">
A right consisting in the absence of a duty: you have a liberty right to do something exactly when you have no duty not to do it.
</Definition>

Hohfeld identified four distinct things the word right is used for, and the two above are the ones that carry most of the weight in political argument. The other two are worth naming. A power is the ability to change someone's rights and duties, which is what a judge has, and what you have when you make a promise. An immunity is the absence of someone else's power over your position, which is what a constitutional guarantee provides.

The most useful result in the whole analysis is that claim rights and duties are two descriptions of one relation. Your claim right that a debt be repaid and the debtor's duty to repay it are not two facts but one fact seen from either end. Nothing is added by asserting the right once the duty is established, and nothing survives the duty's disappearance.

Liberty rights work quite differently and the difference is easy to miss. Saying you have a liberty right to do something says only that you have no duty not to. It imposes no duty on anyone else, including no duty to let you. Two people who spot a banknote on the pavement each have a liberty right to pick it up, neither has a duty to leave it for the other, and each may perfectly properly get there first.

That is why the political question is almost never whether someone has a liberty right. Almost anything you have no duty to refrain from is something you have a liberty right to do, and the interesting question is always whether the liberty is protected by a surrounding claim right that others not interfere. A liberty right on its own is a very thin thing.

## Where rights come from

<Definition term="Natural rights">
Rights held in virtue of what a person is rather than in virtue of any law, agreement, or institution, and therefore held by people whether or not any legal system recognises them.
</Definition>

The contrast is with rights that exist because some institution created them. Your right to vote in a particular election exists because a law says so, and a different law would abolish it. A natural right, if there are any, would not be like that. It would be there before the law, would provide the standard by which laws are assessed, and would be violated rather than cancelled by a law denying it.

Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* of 1689 is the reference point in English, and the tradition it belongs to supplied the vocabulary of the American and French revolutionary declarations. The attraction is obvious. Without something of this kind, the claim that a regime violated its subjects' rights reduces to the claim that it broke its own laws, which is exactly what such regimes usually take care not to do.

The difficulty is equally obvious, which is saying where such rights come from and how anyone knows what they are. The traditional answers appealed to God or to nature, and a secular political philosophy that wants the conclusions without the premises has work to do.

Jeremy Bentham put the objection with more force than anyone since. Writing against the French revolutionary declarations in the 1790s, in a text published in French in 1816 as *Anarchical Fallacies*, he held that a right is the creature of a law and that talk of rights existing prior to law is talk of an effect without its cause. Natural rights he called nonsense, and imprescriptible natural rights nonsense upon stilts. His positive point beneath the abuse was that declarations of natural rights state conclusions without argument: each right is asserted, none is derived, and when two of them conflict the declaration provides no way of deciding between them.

## What a right demands

<Definition term="Negative rights">
Rights whose corresponding duty is a duty of restraint: that others not do something to the right-holder.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Positive rights">
Rights whose corresponding duty is a duty of provision: that others supply the right-holder with something.
</Definition>

A right not to be assaulted is satisfied by everyone leaving you alone. A right to medical treatment is not satisfied by anyone leaving you alone; somebody has to provide the treatment. That is the whole of the distinction, and an enormous amount rides on it.

The case for treating only the negative ones as genuine rights runs as follows. A duty of restraint can be discharged by everyone simultaneously, at no cost, and is perfectly determinate: it tells each person exactly what to do, which is nothing. A duty of provision can be discharged only by someone, at real cost, and the right does not say by whom. If a person has a right to housing, who has failed in their duty when they are homeless? Without an answer the right has no correlative duty, and by Hohfeld's analysis a claim right without a correlative duty is not a claim right at all.

The reply is that the asymmetry is exaggerated and the aside below sets out the strongest version of it.

## Human rights

<Definition term="Human rights">
Rights held by all human beings simply as such, taken to constrain what any state may do to anyone within its power, and asserted in international law and practice independently of any particular legal system's recognition of them.
</Definition>

The modern doctrine is a twentieth-century institution rather than a philosophical position, and it was built deliberately in response to what states had shown themselves willing to do. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in Paris on 10 December 1948, by a vote of forty-eight in favour with eight abstentions and none against.

The Declaration includes both kinds of right, and the political disagreement about that was severe enough to shape everything that followed. When the attempt was made to turn it into binding treaty law, the negative and positive rights were separated into two instruments, adopted together in 1966 and in force from 1976: one covering civil and political rights, the other economic, social and cultural rights. The split tracked the ideological division of the period, with one bloc pressing the first set and the other the second, and the two covenants differ in what they demand: the first requires states to respect and ensure its rights, while the second requires progressive realisation to the maximum of available resources, which is a considerably weaker obligation.

Human rights are related to natural rights and are not the same thing. Natural rights is a claim about the source, holding that the rights exist independently of institutions. Human rights, as the doctrine actually operates, is a claim about scope and universality, and it can be held by someone who thinks the rights are institutional creations that ought to exist everywhere. Whether the doctrine needs a natural-rights foundation to be more than a widely ratified list is one of the central questions of the field, and the advanced tier takes it up.

<Aside title="Whether any right is free">
The clean distinction between rights of restraint and rights of provision does not survive contact with how rights actually work, and Stephen Holmes and Cass Sunstein set out the case in *The Cost of Rights* in 1999.

Consider the most negative-looking right there is: the right not to have your property taken. Satisfying it requires a police force, a system of courts, a land registry, prosecutors, prisons, and a body of law defining what counts as property in the first place. None of that is free, all of it is funded from taxation, and a state that stopped paying for it would find the right unenforced within a season. The right to a fair trial is negative in form and consists almost entirely of provision: a courtroom, a judge, a jury, and in many systems a lawyer at public expense.

Their conclusion was that all rights are positive in the respect that matters, since all of them require a functioning and expensive state, and that the distinction is better understood as one of degree in the visibility of the cost. Rights whose costs appear as a line in a health budget look expensive; rights whose costs appear as the general apparatus of law and order look free, because the expenditure is not itemised against them.

This does not settle the argument in favour of positive rights, and it should not be presented as if it did. Someone can accept every word of it and still hold that there is a moral difference between being required not to attack people and being required to support them. What the argument does remove is the claim that negative rights are cost-free and therefore uniquely unproblematic, and that claim was doing a great deal of the work.
</Aside>

<Objection>
Rights talk has crowded out everything else and made political argument worse.

Framing a claim as a right converts a matter of degree into an absolute. It ends the conversation rather than starting it, since a right by its nature is not to be traded off against considerations of cost or consequence. When both sides frame their positions as rights, as they now invariably do, there is no vocabulary left for the compromise that the situation actually requires.

The inflation is visible in how the list has grown. Once anything worth having can be asserted as a right, the assertion stops carrying weight, and the rights that genuinely matter are devalued by association with claims that are merely preferences dressed up. Ordinary moral concepts that political argument used to have available, obligation and virtue and the common good and fairness, have been squeezed out by a vocabulary that admits only of demands.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The diagnosis has real support and the prescription does not follow from it.

The inflation is genuine and it has a cost. Where a claim is really a matter of degree, calling it a right does obscure that, and the practice of asserting rights as conversation-stoppers has damaged political argument in exactly the way described.

The mistake is treating this as a defect of rights rather than of a particular careless use of them. Rights are not by their nature absolute, and almost no serious theory of rights has ever held that they were. Most hold that rights can be outweighed, that they conflict and must be adjudicated, and that they are constrained by feasibility. That is why legal systems have doctrines about proportionality and about the limits of rights, which would be unintelligible if rights admitted of no weighing. The absolutism is in the rhetoric, not in the concept.

The point about crowding out is fair, and the response is that the other vocabulary should be recovered rather than that this one should be abandoned. There are things that ought to be done which nobody has a right to, and a political discourse that can only recognise claims of entitlement will not see them.

What no version of the objection touches is what rights are for. The point of putting something in the form of a right is that it does not depend on the outcome of a calculation about aggregate benefit, and that it belongs to the individual rather than being contingent on their usefulness. A vocabulary that had only obligation, virtue, and the common good would have no way of saying that a person may not be sacrificed for a general gain, and the twentieth century is what taught people to want a way of saying it.
</Reply>
