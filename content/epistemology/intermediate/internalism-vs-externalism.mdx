A woman has an unusual talent. Shown a photograph of any street in a city she has never visited, she can say correctly whether it is in Lisbon or Porto. She is right every time. She has no idea how she does it, cannot point to anything in the photographs, and if pressed will say that Porto ones just look like Porto. She does not believe she has the talent, because nobody has told her, and she treats each judgement as a guess.

Does she know? The question splits epistemology along its widest fault line, and the two answers are not two theories but two conceptions of what a theory of justification is for.

## The division

<Definition term="Internalism">
The family of views on which whether a belief is justified is settled entirely by factors internal to the believer, so that two people alike in all internal respects are alike in what they are justified in believing.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Externalism">
The denial of internalism. On externalist views, what justifies a belief can include facts the believer has no awareness of, such as the reliability of the process that produced it or the believer's causal relation to what the belief is about.
</Definition>

Stated that way the dispute sounds like a technicality about where to draw a boundary. It is not. Behind the two positions sit two different jobs that the word justification has been asked to do, and much of the literature's stubbornness comes from both jobs being real.

One job is evaluative in a broadly ethical sense. To say a belief is justified is to say the believer is doing well by their own lights, has not been careless, and is not open to criticism for holding it. That job requires the standard to be something the believer could in principle comply with, which pushes towards internalism.

The other job is to explain why justification matters. It matters because justified beliefs tend to be true. A standard that could be met perfectly by a brain in a vat, whose every belief is false, is not obviously tracking anything worth having. That job pushes towards externalism.

## Two ways of being internalist

The internalist family divides over what internal means, and the division matters more than it first appears.

<Definition term="Access internalism">
The view that a factor can justify a belief only if the believer has reflective access to it, so that on reflection alone the believer could become aware of what justifies them.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Epistemic access">
The relation a believer stands in to a justifying factor when that factor is available to them on reflection, without further investigation of the world.
</Definition>

Access internalism is the historically dominant version and the one that gives the position its motivation. Justification is thought of as something a believer can be held to, and one cannot be held to a standard whose satisfaction is invisible.

<Definition term="Mentalism">
The view that justification supervenes on the believer's mental states, so that any two believers in exactly the same mental states are justified in exactly the same beliefs, whether or not those states are reflectively accessible.
</Definition>

Mentalism, defended by Richard Feldman and Earl Conee, is weaker and correspondingly harder to refute. It keeps justification inside the head without requiring that the believer can find it there. A stored memory trace that shapes what seems plausible to you counts as internal on this view even if no amount of reflection will turn it up.

The weakening is a genuine retreat, and it costs something. Access internalism explains why internalism should be true. Mentalism draws the boundary in a place that is easier to defend but leaves the motivating thought behind, since a mental state you cannot access is no more answerable to you than a fact about your visual cortex.

## The case against internalism

The Gettier literature supplies the opening move. If justification is entirely a matter of what is going on inside, then the internal facts about Smith are the same whether or not Jones gets the job, and the same whether Henry is looking at a barn or a facade. Something outside the head is making the difference between knowledge and its absence, and the externalist takes this as evidence that the epistemically relevant properties were never all inside.

The stronger argument is the one about creatures. Small children have knowledge and cannot reflect on their grounds. Animals track their environment reliably and have no grounds to reflect on. Ordinary adults know a great deal that they could not defend if challenged: which way is north in a familiar city, that a friend is upset, that the engine sounds wrong. On access internalism all of this is either not knowledge or rests on reasons the believers do not have.

The woman in the opening case is designed to sharpen this. Her faculty is reliable. Her beliefs are true, and true because of the faculty rather than by accident. What she lacks is any inkling of why she should be believed, including by herself.

<Argument name="The externalist argument from reliability">
  <Premise>What makes justification worth having is its connection to truth.</Premise>
  <Premise>A believer can satisfy every internal condition on justification while their belief-forming processes are systematically unreliable, as in the case of a brain in a vat.</Premise>
  <Premise>A believer can have reliable belief-forming processes while satisfying no internal condition, as in the case of a reliable clairvoyant or of a young child.</Premise>
  <Premise>In each pair it is the reliable believer whose beliefs stand in the relation to truth that matters.</Premise>
  <Conclusion>Justification is constituted at least in part by factors external to the believer.</Conclusion>
</Argument>

## The case against externalism

BonJour's clairvoyance cases, published in 1980, remain the sharpest reply, and they work by making the third premise above cut the other way.

Norman is a reliable clairvoyant. He has no evidence that he has the power, no evidence that anyone does, and no evidence for or against the particular beliefs the power produces. One day he finds himself convinced that the President is in New York, and the President is in New York. By reliabilist lights Norman is justified, since the process is reliable. The intuition BonJour is pumping is that Norman is not justified but irresponsible, and that his conviction ought to strike him as something to be suspicious of rather than something to act on.

The generalisation is that externalism severs justification from responsibility. A believer who has no reason to trust a faculty and trusts it anyway is doing something wrong, and it is no defence that the faculty happens to work. The internalist adds that this is not a minor cost, because the whole point of epistemic evaluation is to guide believers, and a standard nobody can apply guides nobody.

The externalist has two replies. The concessive one is that Norman really is justified and our contrary intuition comes from importing background knowledge that clairvoyance does not exist, which gives Norman a defeater he ought to be responding to. Take that away, and Norman looks like a person with a sense he has not yet named. The unconcessive reply is that guidance was never the job. A theory of justification is a theory of what makes beliefs good, not a manual, and theories of biological function are not criticised for failing to help organisms function.

<Aside title="Why the dispute does not resolve">
It is worth noticing what happened to the word.

Internalists and externalists began as rival answers to one question and have gradually become answers to two. When Alvin Goldman later distinguished weak from strong justification, and when Ernest Sosa distinguished animal from reflective knowledge, both were conceding in effect that the opposing side had identified something real.

Animal knowledge is what the reliable clairvoyant and the young child have: true belief produced by a faculty that works. Reflective knowledge adds a perspective on one's own faculties, an understanding of why they should be trusted. Sosa's suggestion is that these are two grades of achievement and not two competing analyses of one.

That move dissolves the argument at the cost of the original question, which was which of the two is knowledge. The literature has not settled whether this is progress or a change of subject.
</Aside>

## Where this leaves the woman

On mentalism she may be justified, if her sense that Porto looks like Porto counts as a mental state that supports the belief. On access internalism she is not, since reflection turns up nothing. On reliabilism she is justified, and knows. On Sosa's division she has animal knowledge and lacks the reflective kind, which is why she herself treats her judgements as guesses.

The one answer no position gives is that the question is unclear. All four say something definite, and they disagree, which is what a real fault line looks like.

<Objection>
Both sides are measuring the wrong thing. The internalist evaluates the believer and the externalist evaluates the process, and knowledge is neither: it is a relation between a mind and a fact. Asking whether the justifying factor lies inside or outside the skull is like asking whether a marriage is located in one spouse or the other. The boundary of the skin has no epistemological significance, and a dispute organised around it was misconceived from the start.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The complaint about the skin is better than the conclusion drawn from it.

Nobody defends the skull as a principled boundary, and the best versions of internalism do not use it. Mentalism draws the line at the mental rather than the anatomical, which is why extended mind arguments do not immediately refute it, and access internalism draws it at what reflection can reach. Both boundaries are epistemological rather than anatomical.

The relational point is also common ground. Every party agrees that knowledge involves standing in some relation to the world. The dispute is about whether the justification component of that relation is itself relational, and that is a real question with consequences. If it is, then two believers can differ in justification without differing in anything they could notice, and epistemic evaluation stops being something a believer can carry out on themselves. If it is not, then a believer can be perfectly justified while radically out of touch with reality.

The analogy therefore misfires. A marriage is uncontroversially a relation. Whether justification is one is exactly what is at issue.
</Reply>
