Nobody has checked every triangle. Nobody has surveyed the bachelors to confirm that none is married. Yet the claims that the angles of a Euclidean triangle sum to two right angles, and that bachelors are unmarried, seem settled in a way that no amount of further looking would improve.

Contrast the claim that water boils at one hundred degrees at sea level. That one was found out, could have been otherwise, and would be revised if the measurements came out differently. The difference between the two kinds of case is old, and the question of whether it marks a real division is one of the places where epistemology and the philosophy of language meet.

## The distinction

<Definition term="A priori">
Knowledge or justification that does not depend on experience for its warrant. Experience may be needed to acquire the concepts involved, but plays no part in supporting the claim once the concepts are in hand.
</Definition>

<Definition term="A posteriori">
Knowledge or justification that depends on experience for its warrant. Also called empirical knowledge.
</Definition>

Three clarifications keep the distinction from collapsing under the first objection anyone raises.

The distinction is epistemological, not psychological. It concerns what justifies a belief, not how anyone came to have it. A mathematician who arrives at a theorem through a dream and then proves it has a priori justification, and the dream is irrelevant.

Experience is required for concept acquisition on any view. You cannot know that bachelors are unmarried without having learned the words, and learning them took experience. The claim is that once the concepts are possessed, no further experience is consulted. Kant put the point by saying that although all our knowledge begins with experience, it does not follow that it all arises out of experience.

The distinction is not the same as necessary versus contingent, and Saul Kripke's 1970 lectures established that they come apart in both directions. That water is H2O is necessary and known a posteriori. That the standard metre bar is one metre long was, on Kripke's analysis, knowable a priori by whoever fixed the reference, and contingent, since the bar might have been longer.

## What is supposed to do the justifying

<Definition term="Rational intuition">
A non-sensory mental state in which a proposition strikes the thinker as true on the basis of understanding it, offered by rationalists as the source of a priori justification.
</Definition>

The term is unfortunate, since it suggests hunches. What is meant is closer to intellectual seeing. When you consider whether anything can be red and green all over at once, something happens that is not a hunch and is not an inference from observed cases. You grasp that it cannot be.

Laurence BonJour, having abandoned coherentism, spent the later part of his career defending rational insight as a genuine source of justification, on the grounds that no empiricist account can explain our warrant for the principles of inference that empiricism itself uses. George Bealer's version treats intuitions as a basic evidential source with the same standing as perception.

The standard objection is that the account explains nothing. Saying a proposition is justified by rational intuition, when rational intuition is defined as the state of grasping that it is true, comes close to saying that it is justified because it is obvious. The empiricist wants to know what this faculty is, how it could be reliable about abstract matters that do not interact with us causally, and why its deliverances have so often been wrong. The last point carries real weight. Euclidean geometry seemed rationally evident to competent thinkers for two thousand years and is not true of physical space.

## The innateness question

<Definition term="Innate knowledge">
Knowledge held to be present in the mind prior to any experience, rather than acquired from it. The traditional rationalist doctrine, argued for by Plato and Descartes and revived in altered form by Chomsky.
</Definition>

The doctrine has a famous first appearance. In the *Meno*, Socrates draws geometrical results from an untaught slave boy using only questions, and concludes that the boy is recollecting what his soul knew before birth. The argument is memorable, and the conclusion does not follow: the questions do most of the work, and the demonstration shows that the boy can reason, not that he knew.

Locke's attack in the *Essay* was decisive against the version he targeted. If there were principles universally assented to, children and the cognitively impaired would assent to them, and they do not. Leibniz's reply in the *New Essays* was that innate knowledge is dispositional rather than occurrent, present as a tendency that experience triggers, and the image he uses is of veins in a block of marble that dispose it to be split one way rather than another.

The contemporary descendant is not a theory of knowledge but of language. Chomsky's argument from the poverty of the stimulus holds that the linguistic input available to a child radically underdetermines the grammar the child acquires, so that substantial structure must be contributed by the learner. Whether this is innate knowledge in the epistemologist's sense is contested, since an innate constraint on hypothesis formation is not obviously a justified true belief, and Chomsky himself has been careful about the claim.

<Argument name="The argument from necessity">
  <Premise>Some propositions are known to be necessarily true, such as that nothing is both round and square.</Premise>
  <Premise>Experience informs us about how things actually are, not about how they must be, since observing many cases never shows that a case of the opposite is impossible.</Premise>
  <Premise>So experience cannot be what justifies knowledge of necessity.</Premise>
  <Conclusion>Knowledge of necessary truths, if there is any, is a priori.</Conclusion>
</Argument>

The argument is the strongest one in the rationalist's hands, and the empiricist has two ways to resist. The first is to deny the first premise and hold that apparent necessities are deeply entrenched empirical generalisations, which is Quine's route and the subject of the next article. The second is to grant the conclusion while draining it of interest, by holding that necessary truths are true by convention or by meaning, so that knowing them requires only knowing the language.

<Aside title="Kant's third option">
Kant accepted that the empiricists were right about experience and that the rationalists were right about necessity, and concluded that both had misdescribed the alternatives.

His innovation was to separate the a priori question from the analytic question, which had been run together. A judgement is analytic if the predicate is contained in the subject concept, and synthetic otherwise. Cross this with the a priori and a posteriori distinction and there are four boxes, of which three were uncontroversial. The fourth, the synthetic a priori, is where he located mathematics, the principle that every event has a cause, and much else.

The explanation he gave is the transcendental one. These propositions are knowable a priori and are not merely definitional because they describe the structure that any possible experience must have, a structure the mind contributes rather than finds. We can know in advance that experience will be spatial and temporal and causally ordered, because those are the forms under which experience is possible for us at all.

Whether the explanation works is a question for the metaphysics ladder. What matters here is the shape of the move. Kant does not defend a faculty of rational insight into a realm of abstract objects. He relocates the necessity into the conditions of experience, which is why the position is neither rationalism nor empiricism as either side had stated it.
</Aside>

## The state of play

Almost nobody now holds the strong rationalist position that substantive truths about the world are knowable by pure reason. Almost nobody holds the strong empiricist position either, because logic and mathematics remain stubborn: the claim that they are empirical generalisations has never been made plausible in detail, and the claim that they are empty of content has its own difficulties.

Where the field has settled, to the extent it has, is on a modest a priori. There is justification that does not rest on experience, it covers logic, mathematics, and conceptual truths, it is defeasible rather than certain, and its epistemology remains unclear. That last clause is not a footnote. A source of justification whose workings nobody can explain is exactly what the empiricist tradition objected to, and calling it modest does not dispose of the objection.

<Objection>
The distinction cannot be drawn because the notion of experience doing no work is incoherent. Every judgement, including the most abstract, is made by a physical brain that was shaped by experience, using concepts acquired through experience, and applying standards of good reasoning learned from a community. Peel away everything experience contributed and nothing is left to do the judging. The a priori is a description of how certain judgements feel from the inside, not a category of justification.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The argument proves too much, and seeing why locates the real issue.

The same considerations apply to perception. A perceptual judgement is made by a brain shaped by evolution and training, using concepts acquired socially, and applying learned standards. If that genetic story dissolves the a priori, it dissolves the distinction between justified and unjustified belief altogether, since every judgement has such a story. The rationalist's claim was never that some judgements have no causal history, which would be absurd, but that experience does not figure among the grounds. A claim about grounds is not refuted by facts about causes.

What the objection does press successfully is the reliability question. If the faculty that delivers a priori judgements is a product of contingent history, there is a real question why it should be accurate about necessity, and the answer cannot be that it seems accurate to itself. Empirical work on the variability of philosophical intuitions has given this question teeth that it did not have when BonJour was writing.

So the distinction survives and the confidence does not. Calling a judgement a priori says where its warrant does not come from. It does not say that the warrant is good.
</Reply>
