In Plato's *Meno*, Socrates asks why knowledge should be prized above true opinion. Someone who knows the road to Larissa and someone who merely has a correct belief about it will both get there. If both arrive, what does knowledge add?

Socrates' answer is that true opinions are like the statues of Daedalus, which were said to run away unless tied down. A true belief without knowledge may be dislodged by the next clever argument. Knowledge is true belief tied down by an account of the reason why, and is therefore more stable.

The question lay mostly dormant for two thousand years. It returned at the end of the twentieth century with a sharper edge, as an objection to a particular theory of knowledge. If reliabilism is right, it seemed, knowledge could not be worth any more than true belief at all.

## Kinds of value

<Definition term="Epistemic value">
The kind of value that belongs to cognitive states and achievements in virtue of their relation to cognitive goals such as truth, understanding and knowledge, as opposed to their practical, moral or aesthetic value.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Instrumental value">
The value something has as a means to something else. Money has instrumental value because of what it can buy. A reliable thermometer has instrumental value because it tends to give accurate readings.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Final value">
The value something has for its own sake, as an end rather than as a means. Final value is sometimes called intrinsic value, though the two can come apart: something might be valued for its own sake because of a relational property, such as being the first edition of a book.
</Definition>

The distinction matters because the question "why is knowledge more valuable?" can be heard in two ways. Is it more useful? Or is it better in itself? The Meno reading is about usefulness: knowledge gets you to Larissa more dependably. The contemporary puzzle is about the other kind.

## Veritism

<Definition term="Veritism">
The view that truth is the fundamental epistemic good, so that all other epistemic goods, including justification, reliability and rationality, are valuable only as means to true belief and the avoidance of false belief. The term is Alvin Goldman's.
</Definition>

Veritism is the natural companion of reliabilism. If a reliable process is good because it tends to produce truths, then its epistemic value lies in the truths it produces. Justification is valuable because it tracks truth. Rationality is valuable because rational methods tend to get things right. The one thing valued for its own sake is truth itself.

This is elegant. It explains why we care about evidence, careful reasoning and good instruments: because they lead to truth. It explains why we criticise wishful thinking: because it does not. And it gives epistemology a single aim in the way consequentialist ethics gives morality one.

## The swamping problem

<Definition term="Swamping problem">
The objection, pressed by Linda Zagzebski, Jonathan Kvanvig and others, that if the value of a reliable process lies wholly in the true beliefs it produces, then a true belief produced by a reliable process has no more value than the same true belief produced by an unreliable one. The value of the truth swamps the value of the reliability.
</Definition>

Zagzebski's version, from 2003, uses a cup of coffee.

A good espresso machine is valuable because it tends to make good espresso. It is reliable. But suppose you have a cup of excellent espresso in front of you. Does it taste better, or is it more valuable, for having come from a reliable machine rather than a temperamental one that happened to produce a good cup? It does not. Once the espresso exists, the reliability of its source adds nothing to it. The value of the machine is exhausted by the value of the cups it makes, and a particular good cup is just as good whatever made it.

<Argument name="The swamping argument">
  <Premise>Knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable process.</Premise>
  <Premise>The epistemic value of a reliable process is wholly instrumental: it is valuable only as a means to true belief.</Premise>
  <Premise>If the value of a property of a thing is wholly instrumental to some good, and the thing already has that good, then the property adds no further value to it.</Premise>
  <Premise>A reliably formed true belief already has the good of truth.</Premise>
  <Conclusion>A reliably formed true belief is no more epistemically valuable than an unreliably formed true belief. So knowledge, on the reliabilist account, is no more valuable than true belief.</Conclusion>
</Argument>

The argument does not deny that reliable processes are valuable. They are, since they produce more truths. The claim is narrower: the reliability of the source cannot make a given true belief more valuable than it would otherwise be. And since we think knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief, the reliabilist has a problem.

The argument generalises. It threatens any theory on which knowledge is true belief plus a property whose value is instrumental to truth. That is why it is sometimes put as a problem for veritism rather than for reliabilism in particular.

## Responses

### The conditional probability solution

Alvin Goldman and Erik Olsson replied in 2009 with what they called the conditional probability solution. A true belief produced by a reliable process makes it more probable that future beliefs of the same kind will be true. If you know the road to Larissa, you are likely to know the road next time too, and the time after. The reliably formed belief is a sign of a reliable faculty, and that faculty will keep producing truths.

The coffee analogy does not capture this, because a good cup of espresso says nothing about the next cup. But a reliably formed true belief does say something about the future. It raises the probability that your next belief will also be true.

Critics reply that this explains why it is useful to have reliable faculties, and why it is good to have a history of reliably formed beliefs, but not why a given item of knowledge is better than the corresponding true belief considered on its own. It is also unclear that the solution handles cases in which no future beliefs of the same type will be formed.

### Credit and achievement

A more influential response comes from virtue epistemology. Ernest Sosa, John Greco and Zagzebski herself argued that knowledge is a kind of achievement: true belief that is creditable to the believer's intellectual virtue. Achievements have final value. We admire the archer who hits the target through skill more than the one who hits it by a lucky gust of wind, even if the arrow ends up in the same place.

If knowledge is an achievement, its value does not come entirely from the truth of the belief. It comes from the fact that the truth was reached through the believer's competence. That is a relational property of the belief, and relational properties can carry final value. The swamping argument's third premise fails, because the virtue that produces knowledge is not valuable only as a means to truth.

The credit view faces an objection from testimony. When you ask a stranger for directions and believe them, you seem to know where the station is, but little of the credit for getting it right belongs to you. It belongs to the stranger. Jennifer Lackey pressed this objection, and virtue epistemologists have responded by weakening the credit requirement to allow shared or partial credit.

### Abandoning the premise

A third response, associated with Kvanvig, is to accept the swamping argument's conclusion and relocate the value. Kvanvig argued that knowledge is not, in fact, distinctively valuable over true belief plus justification, and that the thing we really prize is understanding: grasping how various truths hang together and why things are as they are. Understanding cannot be reduced to knowledge of a set of propositions, and it has a value that knowledge lacks.

This is an unusual move, since it treats the value problem as showing that epistemologists have focused on the wrong target. But it has become influential, and the study of understanding is now a significant subfield.

<Aside title="Pluralism about epistemic value">
The swamping problem depends on veritism: the claim that truth is the only fundamental epistemic good. Several philosophers have argued that this is false.

Pluralists hold that there are several epistemic goods, possibly including knowledge, understanding, wisdom, and coherence, and that none reduces to the others. On a pluralist view, the value of knowledge does not have to be derived from the value of truth, so it cannot be swamped by it.

Pluralism has its own cost. Veritism offered a unified account of why we care about evidence and justification: they lead to truth. Pluralism gives up that unity and must explain each epistemic good separately, as well as how they trade off against one another when they conflict. The accuracy-first programme, treated two articles on, is a return to veritism in a precise and formal form, and it inherits both veritism's elegance and its exposure to the swamping problem.
</Aside>

## The Meno question again

Socrates' answer was about stability. That answer was set aside in the recent literature, but it has made a comeback. Timothy Williamson argued that knowledge is more robust than true belief: someone who knows is less likely to lose their belief in the face of misleading evidence than someone who merely believes truly. This is a practical advantage, and it does not face the swamping problem, since robustness is a feature the knower has that the lucky believer lacks, not a feature of the source that affects only the belief's truth.

This returns the discussion to the instrumental reading, and it might be that the Meno question has two answers. Knowledge is more useful because it is more stable. Whether it is also finally more valuable depends on whether one accepts the credit view.

<Objection>
The swamping problem trades on an analogy that fails. A cup of coffee is a separate object from the machine that produced it, so the machine's reliability is extrinsic to it. But a belief is not separate from the process that produced it. A belief's having been reliably formed is part of what that belief is, just as a painting's having been painted by Rembrandt is part of what makes it valuable. The argument ignores the difference between products and states.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The objection points to something real. Provenance does matter to value in some cases: a genuine Rembrandt is worth more than a perfect forgery. If a belief's provenance can confer value in the same way, the swamping argument's third premise is false.

But the reliabilist cannot easily use this reply. The Rembrandt example works because we value the painting partly as an expression of Rembrandt's skill, which is a form of achievement. The reliabilist's process is not an achievement in that sense; it is a mechanism whose value, by the reliabilist's own lights, lies entirely in its output. To say that reliable provenance confers final value, the reliabilist must say that reliability is valuable for some reason other than its tendency to produce truths. That is to give up veritism, and at that point the view has moved to the credit theory the objection was trying to avoid.
</Reply>
