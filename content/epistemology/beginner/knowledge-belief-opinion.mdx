Two people say the coin will land heads. One has watched a slow-motion recording of the toss. The other simply feels lucky. The coin lands heads, and both were right. Something separates them, and saying what it is turns out to be the work of a whole subject.

## What is being analysed

The word "know" covers several things, and only one of them is under examination here.

<Definition term="Propositional knowledge">
Knowing that something is the case: that Ottawa is the capital of Canada, that the bridge will hold, that the patient has an infection. It takes a complete statement as its object rather than a person, a place, or a skill.
</Definition>

Two other uses of the word are set aside rather than denied. Knowing how to swim is a capacity rather than a stored fact, and Gilbert Ryle argued that it cannot be reduced to knowing a set of propositions about swimming, since one can recite the propositions and sink. Knowing a person or a city is familiarity built by acquaintance, which Bertrand Russell distinguished from knowledge of truths about them.

Both are real and neither is the topic. When epistemologists ask what knowledge is, they mean the first sort, and the analyses proposed are analyses of the form "S knows that P".

## The three notions

<Definition term="Belief">
Taking something to be the case. To believe that P is to hold P as true, which is compatible with being wrong, being unsure, and never having examined the matter.
</Definition>

Belief in this sense has nothing to do with religion or with conviction. It is the ordinary state of accepting something, and most of what anyone believes was absorbed rather than decided. You believe there is a floor below you now, and you did not arrive at that by reasoning.

<Definition term="Truth">
The property a statement has when things are as it says they are. "Snow is white" is true when snow is white.
</Definition>

The definition is deliberately thin. What makes a statement true, and whether truth consists in correspondence to fact or in some other relation, is a large question that the philosophy of language takes up. Nothing in this tier depends on the answer. What matters here is only that truth is a property of what is believed rather than of the believer, and that whether a belief is true is not settled by how confident the believer is.

<Definition term="Opinion">
A belief held on grounds the holder does not regard as settling the matter, and about which reasonable disagreement is expected.
</Definition>

Opinion is not a third thing alongside belief. It is a belief with a particular standing: something held provisionally, or in an area where the holder does not expect everyone competent to agree. Note that opinions can be true. The person who feels lucky about the coin held an opinion, and it was correct.

<Definition term="Knowledge">
Belief that has both the required relation to truth and something further that a lucky guess lacks. What that further thing is, is the question the rest of this tier and the next builds toward.
</Definition>

The definition is openly incomplete, and any honest one at this stage will be. What can be fixed now is two constraints that survive every account. Knowledge requires truth: nobody knows something false, and finding out that a claim is false retracts the claim to have known it rather than merely revising it. And knowledge requires more than accidentally hitting the truth.

<Aside title="The classroom division of fact and opinion">
Schoolwork often asks students to sort statements into facts and opinions, with "the Earth orbits the Sun" on one side and "chocolate is delicious" on the other. The exercise mixes up two different divisions and creates a persistent muddle.

One division is between statements that are true or false and statements that report a preference. Another is between matters where competent people agree and matters where they do not. These come apart in both directions. Whether a given tax cut increased employment is true or false, and competent people disagree about it. Whether a chess position is winning is a matter of preference to nobody, and it is also often disputed.

Calling something an opinion in ordinary speech usually reports the second division and gets heard as the first, with the result that "that's just your opinion" is treated as showing that no answer exists. It shows no such thing.
</Aside>

## The Meno's question

Plato put the difficulty in a form that has not been improved on. In the *Meno*, Socrates observes that a man who has a correct opinion about the road to Larissa will guide you there as reliably as a man who knows the road. On the journey itself, the two are worth exactly the same.

So why is knowledge better? The answer offered compares true opinions to the statues of Daedalus, which in the story run away unless they are tied down. True opinions are like that: correct while they last, and liable to be talked out of, forgotten, or abandoned at the first contrary suggestion, because nothing holds them in place. Tie one down with an account of why it is so, and it becomes stable. In the Lamb translation, "this is why knowledge is more prized than right opinion: the one transcends the other by its trammels".

Whether the tethering account is right, the diagnosis is one that survived. The difference between knowing and being right is a difference in what supports the belief rather than in the belief's content, and the two people betting on the coin held the same view about the same coin.

## Is belief really required

<Objection>
Knowledge does not require belief at all. Consider a student who was taught English history as a child and thinks he has forgotten all of it. Asked when the Battle of Hastings was, he says he is only guessing, and answers 1066. Asked several more questions, he answers each correctly. He denies believing any of his answers, and he sincerely means it. He nonetheless produced the information reliably, which is what knowing is for.
</Objection>

<Reply>
Colin Radford built this case, and the response most epistemologists give is that the example shows something about confidence rather than about belief.

Believing something is not the same as being sure of it or being prepared to say so. The examinee's behaviour is exactly what belief predicts: he produces one answer rather than another, and he produces the right one repeatedly. What he lacks is confidence in himself as a source, which is a second-order matter about his own reliability and not a failure to take 1066 as the answer.

The case does establish something worth keeping. Belief is shown by what a person does with a claim, not by what the person says about how sure they are, and a test that asks people to report their confidence will misclassify cases like this one.
</Reply>
