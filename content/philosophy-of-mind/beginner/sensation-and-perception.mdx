Put a coin on the table and look at it from the side. It is round and it looks elliptical.

Nothing about that sentence is controversial and almost every position in the philosophy of perception has to explain it. The explanations turn out to differ so sharply that a large part of the subject can be reconstructed from this one observation, and the first thing to come under pressure is the ordinary belief that when you look at a coin you see a coin.

## The state under examination

<Definition term="Perceptual experience">
The conscious episode a subject undergoes when they perceive, or seem to perceive, their surroundings: how things look, sound, or feel to them on an occasion.
</Definition>

Two features of the definition are doing work.

The first is the phrase "or seem to". A perceptual experience is picked out by its character rather than by its success, so a person taken in by a convincing fake and a person seeing the real thing can be having experiences of the same type. Whether they really are is the question the rest of this article circles.

The second is that experience is not belief. You can see the coin as elliptical while believing it is round, and the belief does not correct the look. This is easiest to check with a standard geometrical illusion: measure two lines, confirm they are the same length, and they continue to look different. Whatever perceptual experience is, it is not a matter of what you take to be the case, since it survives your taking the opposite.

<Definition term="Sensation">
The raw registering of a stimulus, considered apart from any recognition or classification of what it is: a patch of colour, a pressure, a sound, before it is taken as anything in particular.
</Definition>

The traditional division puts sensation at the bottom and perception above it, with sensation delivering unstructured material and perception organising it into a world of objects. Seeing a red patch is sensation; seeing a tomato is perception.

The division is useful and it should be held loosely, because a great deal of evidence suggests that no such stage exists on its own. Perception is organised from very early on, and what is delivered to consciousness is already structured into surfaces and objects rather than assembled from a mosaic afterward. Nobody has managed to point to a moment of pure sensation in an adult perceiver, and some philosophers hold there is no such thing.

Even so, the distinction marks a real difference in what a theory is trying to explain. A question about how a system detects a wavelength is a different question from how it comes to be seeing a tomato, and running them together produces confusion in both directions.

## The trouble starts with ordinary cases

Return to the coin. It is round, and it looks elliptical, and now a premise gets introduced that looks harmless.

If something looks a certain way to you, then there is something you are aware of that is that way. In the literature this is the phenomenal principle, and its appeal is that it seems to be the only way to take appearances seriously. If nothing elliptical were involved anywhere, what would make it true that something looked elliptical rather than triangular?

Apply the principle to the coin and you get a result at once. Something elliptical is present to your awareness. The coin is not elliptical. So the thing you are aware of is not the coin.

<Definition term="The argument from illusion">
The argument that since illusory experiences present objects as having qualities they do not have, the immediate object of awareness in illusion is not the ordinary object; and since illusory and veridical experiences are of the same kind, the immediate object of awareness is never the ordinary object.
</Definition>

The second half is where the argument becomes ambitious, and it is the half most people miss on first reading. Establishing something about illusions alone would be a minor result. The argument gets its reach from a further claim, that veridical and illusory experiences do not differ in kind, so that whatever account is given of one has to be given of the other.

That claim is not arbitrary. The experiences can be indistinguishable from the inside, and the ordinary case shades continuously into the illusory one as you tilt the coin. Drawing a line anywhere on that continuum and saying that awareness has a different object on either side of it looks unmotivated. If you are aware of something elliptical when the coin is tilted a lot, it is hard to say what changes when it is tilted a little.

The versions of this argument were developed carefully in the early twentieth century by G. E. Moore and Bertrand Russell, and it was set out in the form best known to later readers by A. J. Ayer in 1940. Russell's own route ran through variation rather than error: a table looks different in colour and shape from every position and in every light, no one of those appearances has a better claim than the others to be how the table is, and the table itself does not change as you walk around it.

<Definition term="Sense datum">
An immediate object of perceptual awareness, understood as actually having the qualities the experience presents: a coloured, shaped item of which the perceiver is directly aware and which is distinct from the physical object.
</Definition>

The term was introduced into English philosophy by Moore in a paper read to the Aristotelian Society at the end of 1909, and Russell adopted it. The theory built on it holds that we are directly aware of sense data and only indirectly aware of physical objects, which we know about by inference or construction from the data. It was the dominant account of perception for several decades, and the intermediate tier examines it properly along with the objections that eventually sank it.

Three of those objections are worth previewing, since they show why the position is a costly one. Sense data do not fit anywhere in the physical world, being neither in the head nor out where the coin is, so a physicalist who accepts them owes an account of what they are. They also seem to have indeterminate properties in a way ordinary objects cannot: a speckled hen seen briefly presents an experience with speckles that has no definite number of them, and an object with no definite number of speckles is a strange item. And positing them puts a screen between the perceiver and the world, which raises the question of how anyone ever knew about the coin.

## Hallucination is worse

<Definition term="Hallucination">
An experience that is subjectively like perceiving an ordinary object, but in which no such object is being perceived.
</Definition>

Illusion involves a real object misrepresented. Hallucination involves no object at all, and it produces a parallel argument that does not need the phenomenal principle to get going.

In a hallucination there is no ordinary object being perceived, so whatever the subject is aware of, it is not one. If a hallucination can be indistinguishable from a veridical perception, and there is no evident reason it could not be, then the two experiences are of the same kind. And if they are of the same kind, the account given of the hallucination has to be given of the perception too.

This version is the sharper of the two, because it cannot be met by denying that anything really is elliptical. There is simply nothing there, and the subject is still undergoing something with a full perceptual character.

The step that has attracted the most scrutiny is the move from indistinguishability to sameness of kind, sometimes called the common kind assumption. Two things being impossible to tell apart from the inside does not obviously make them the same sort of thing, and a family of positions developed in the last few decades denies exactly this, holding that a genuine perception and a matching hallucination are fundamentally different states that happen to be introspectively indiscriminable. The intermediate tier takes that up.

<Aside title="Where the options lie">
Four broad responses are on the table and it helps to have the map before meeting them individually.

One accepts sense data: there is an inner item with the presented qualities, and perceiving the world is a matter of standing in some relation to it.

A second denies the object and keeps the quality, holding that to have an experience as of something elliptical is not to be aware of an elliptical thing but to be experiencing in a certain manner. The trick is that the ellipticality attaches to the experiencing rather than to any item experienced, which avoids the awkward inner object at the cost of a way of speaking that many find hard to make sense of.

A third treats perceptual experience as representational, on the model of the contentful states of the previous article. The experience represents the coin as elliptical, and a representation can misrepresent without there being anything that is the way it is represented, in just the way that a false belief needs no false fact to be about. This has been the most popular option in recent decades.

The fourth rejects the common kind assumption and holds that in the good case the perceiver is directly related to the object itself, with the bad cases being a different kind of state altogether.

Each of these buys relief from the arguments above at a price, and the arguments over which price is worth paying are the content of the intermediate and advanced tiers.
</Aside>

<Objection>
J. L. Austin pressed the case that the argument is a conjuring trick. It runs together cases that have nothing to do with each other under the single word illusion: a straight stick looking bent in water, a coin looking elliptical, mirages, dreams, and mirror images are not one phenomenon, and most of them are not illusions at all. The coin looks exactly as a round coin should look from that angle, and a round coin that looked circular from every angle would be the astonishing thing. Nobody is deceived and nothing has gone wrong.

The argument also leans on a contrast between direct and indirect perception that has never been given a clear sense. We know what it is to see something indirectly, in a mirror or on a screen, and that ordinary use provides no foothold for the claim that we never see anything directly. The philosopher's use of the word is a technical coinage dressed up as ordinary language, and the plain fact is that we see coins.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The criticism landed hard and it changed how the subject is done. Austin's lectures were published in 1962 as *Sense and Sensibilia*, and the confident sense-datum theorising of the previous generation did not survive them intact. Two of his charges are simply correct: the cases are heterogeneous, and the perspectival appearance of a coin is not an error about anything.

What it does not do is remove the problem, for two reasons.

The first is that the argument from hallucination is untouched by all of it. Whatever is going on when a coin looks elliptical, there are cases in which a person has a fully perceptual experience and nothing is there. The demand for an account of what is happening in those cases is not a demand created by philosophers' misuse of the word illusion.

The second is that the phenomenal principle is a claim about awareness rather than about ordinary language, and pointing out that ordinary speakers do not call the coin case an illusion leaves it standing. Someone who thinks the principle is false owes a positive account of what makes it true that things look ways they are not, and giving that account is precisely the work the third and fourth options in the aside above are doing. Austin's contribution is best read as clearing away a bad argument for a position, rather than as showing that the position it argued for was unnecessary.

The lasting effect is a demand for care. Illusion, hallucination, dreaming, and perspectival variation should be handled separately and shown to raise the same difficulty if they do, rather than assumed to be one topic because one word covers them.
</Reply>
