Two people argue about whether it was wrong to break the promise. A third says that nothing is ever really wrong, and that both of them are expressing preferences in a grammar that disguises them as reports.

The third person has not joined the argument. They have said something about the argument, and about what the two participants were doing when they had it. Sorting out which question is which is the last piece of vocabulary this tier supplies.

## Two levels

<Definition term="Normative ethics">
The part of ethics that asks which actions are right, which things are good, and what makes them so. Its output is claims about conduct.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Metaethics">
The part of ethics that asks about moral claims rather than making them: what they mean, whether they can be true, what would make them true, and how anyone could know. Its output is claims about morality.
</Definition>

Everything in this tier so far has been normative ethics or preparation for it. The theories of the fourth article are answers to which considerations make actions right. The trolley pair tests those answers. The method of the sixth article is how such answers are supported.

Metaethics steps back from all of that. It takes the practice of arguing about right and wrong as its subject and asks what the practice involves. A metaethicist can hold any first-order view or none, and two people who agree completely about which actions are wrong can disagree completely at this level.

Notice that stepping back is not neutrality. Saying that moral claims are expressions of attitude rather than reports of fact is a substantive and contestable claim, not a refusal to take sides.

## The first question: what are we saying?

<Definition term="Moral semantics">
The study of what moral claims mean and what kind of speech act is performed in making them: whether they state facts, express attitudes, issue prescriptions, or do something else.
</Definition>

"Torturing children for entertainment is wrong" looks grammatically like "arsenic is poisonous". Both are declarative, both take a subject and a predicate, both can be embedded in conditionals and questioned and denied.

Whether the resemblance is real is the question. On one family of views the sentence states something that is straightforwardly true or false, and morality is a subject matter about which people have beliefs. On a rival family the surface grammar is misleading, and the sentence functions to express an attitude of disapproval or to prescribe rather than to describe. The intermediate tier calls these families cognitivism and non-cognitivism and works through the main versions of each, along with the hardest objection to the second, which is that it struggles to explain what moral sentences are doing when they appear inside conditionals and arguments.

## The second question: is there anything there?

<Definition term="Moral metaphysics">
The study of what would have to exist for moral claims to be true, and whether it does: whether there are moral properties and facts, and what sort of thing they could be.
</Definition>

Grant for the sake of argument that moral claims do state facts. Then the fact that torture is wrong is a fact about something, and an account is owed of what wrongness is and where it fits.

The options in play range widely. Some hold that moral properties are natural properties, identical with or constituted by facts about wellbeing or harm, and therefore no more mysterious than facts about health. Some hold that they are real and irreducible, a distinct kind of feature that the natural sciences do not describe. Some hold that moral claims do state facts and that all the positive ones are false, since the world contains nothing of the kind they are about, a position J. L. Mackie defended in 1977 partly on the grounds that objective values would have to be entities of a very strange sort. And some hold that moral facts exist but are constituted by the output of a procedure of reasoning rather than found. The intermediate and advanced tiers take each of these in turn.

## The third question: how could anyone tell?

<Definition term="Moral epistemology">
The study of how moral claims could be known or justified, and what the sources of moral knowledge are.
</Definition>

The previous article was already doing this, without the label. Asking whether an intuition is evidence is asking a question in moral epistemology, and the method of reflective equilibrium is a proposed answer to it.

The area is harder than its counterpart elsewhere because the obvious source is missing. Claims about the natural world are checked against observation, and there is no moral observation. Nobody sees the wrongness of an act in the way they see its speed. What is left is intuition, inference from other moral claims, and inference from non-moral facts, and each has been challenged. The persistence of moral disagreement is standardly raised here as evidence that whatever the sources are, they are not working well, and the intermediate tier examines whether that argument succeeds.

## A fourth strand

Running through the three is a set of questions about the relation between moral judgement and motivation. Someone who sincerely judges that they ought to keep a promise seems to be at least somewhat motivated to keep it, and a person who says they judge it and feels nothing invites the suspicion that they do not really judge it.

If that connection is a necessary one, it constrains everything above. A view on which moral judgements are beliefs about ordinary facts owes an explanation of why this particular belief comes with motivation attached, since beliefs about the ordinary world generally do not. A view on which they are expressions of attitude gets the connection for nothing and pays for it elsewhere. The intermediate tier treats this under the Humean theory of motivation, and it is where the semantic and metaphysical questions turn out to have teeth.

<Aside title="The questions are not independent">
The four are separated for exposition and they constrain each other tightly, which is what makes the field a field rather than a list.

An answer in semantics restricts the metaphysics. If moral sentences do not state facts, the question of what makes them true does not arise in the ordinary way, and a position that started as a claim about meaning has settled a question about what exists.

An answer in metaphysics restricts the epistemology. If moral properties are natural properties, moral knowledge is empirical knowledge of a specialised kind and the sources are the ordinary ones. If they are irreducible and non-natural, some other faculty has to be doing the work, and saying what it is has been the standing difficulty for that position.

And the epistemology pushes back on the metaphysics. A view on which moral facts exist but are in principle undetectable is a view on which nobody has ever had a justified moral belief, which most philosophers treat as a reason to reject the metaphysics rather than to accept the consequence.
</Aside>

<Objection>
None of this changes anything. Whatever the correct account of what moral sentences mean, the same acts remain intolerable, and no metaethical result has ever given anyone a reason to act differently. A person convinced that moral claims express attitudes goes on condemning cruelty exactly as before, and is right to. The field is a hobby that has been detached from its subject.
</Objection>

<Reply>
The independence is real and is a point in the field's favour rather than against it.

That first-order commitments survive metaethical results is precisely what defenders of the deflationary positions have argued. An expressivist maintains that a person who thinks moral claims express attitudes can and should keep every one of their moral convictions, including the conviction that cruelty is wrong regardless of anyone's attitude. If that is right, the objection has described the situation accurately and drawn the wrong conclusion from it.

Two kinds of practical consequence do get through, and they are not small.

The first concerns disagreement. What you take yourself to be doing when you argue with someone about a moral question depends on what you think the question is. Whether persistent disagreement between careful people is evidence that at least one of them is wrong, or evidence that there is nothing to be wrong about, is a metaethical question with an immediate bearing on how such arguments should be conducted.

The second concerns authority. Moral claims are used to justify coercion: laws, punishment, intervention. The question of what entitles anyone to impose a moral standard on someone who rejects it cannot be answered from within normative ethics, because the person who rejects it also rejects the theory. It is a question about what moral claims are and what would make them true, and the political-philosophy ladder needs an answer to it.
</Reply>

## What this tier has done

Seven articles have supplied vocabulary rather than conclusions, which is what a beginner tier owes.

Claims about what is were separated from claims about what ought to be, and the ought was analysed into contributory reasons and all-things-considered verdicts. A moral theory was distinguished from a list of verdicts, and from the decision procedure it may or may not recommend. Three families of theory were set against a single case and shown to disagree about the question before they disagree about the answer. A designed pair of cases exposed the distinctions that deontological theories rest on, and the difficulty of stating a principle that yields both verdicts. The method that all of this relies on was named and its main objection stated. And the questions about morality were separated from the questions within it.

The intermediate tier takes the theories one at a time with their developed objections, works through the metaethical positions named here, and takes up motivation and reasons, which is where the two levels meet.
